\## \*\*Research Anchors\*\*



\*\*Narrow Focus: World War 2 (1933-1945)\*\*



\### \*\*Campaigns (6)\*\*



\* \*\*Eastern Front attrition and logistics:\*\* the chunk is substantially about the grinding, resource-draining nature of the German-Soviet war (1941-45), including but not limited to manpower losses, supply lines, distance and weather, and industrial replacement capacity, rather than the tactical narrative of a single battle. Siege or encirclement operations count only if the passage focuses on their attritional or logistical effect.  

\* \*\*Siege and encirclement warfare:\*\* the chunk is substantially about large-scale sieges and encirclement campaigns on any front of the war, including but not limited to those typically involving hundreds of thousands of troops and ending in mass casualties or the capture of large numbers of soldiers and prisoners of war.  

\* \*\*Pacific island and amphibious campaigns:\*\* the chunk is substantially about military campaigns in the Pacific theater (1941-45), including but not limited to Japan’s offensive conquests and the U.S. island-hopping advance, in which forces took territory by amphibious assault or defended it against one.  

\* \*\*Battle of the Atlantic and convoy warfare:\*\* the chunk is substantially about the naval struggle (1939-45) over Allied shipping across the Atlantic, including but not limited to German U-boats and surface raiders attacking merchant convoys carrying supplies to Britain and the Soviet Union, and Allied navies, merchant crews, and aircraft working to protect them.  

\* \*\*Strategic bombing as an operational campaign:\*\* the chunk is substantially about sustained air campaigns against enemy cities, industry, and infrastructure (1939-45), including but not limited to the Blitz, the Allied bombing of Germany, and the firebombing of Japan, focusing on planning, targets, forces, and operational results, rather than close air support for ground troops or artillery bombardment.  

\* \*\*D-Day and the Normandy landings:\*\* the chunk is substantially about the Allied invasion of Normandy (June 1944\\) as the opening of a second front in Western Europe, including but not limited to its operational objectives, the strategic planning and breakdown of the landings, intelligence support specific to the invasion (French Resistance assistance to the Allies, the deception operation that led the Germans to expect an attack at Calais), Stalin’s interest in the second front as it relates to the invasion, and the results of the landings.



&#x20; ### \*\*Politics \& Diplomacy (7)\*\*



\* \*\*Appeasement of Germany in the 1930s:\*\* This chunk is substantially focused on the policy of appeasement adopted by the future Allies towards Hitler’s efforts to flout the Versailles Treaty, including but not limited to rearmament and treaty renegotiation, a desire for Lebensraum, and aggressive actions taken towards countries such as Austria, Czechoslovakia, etc.  

\* \*\*Munich Agreement and Czechoslovakia:\*\* This chunk is substantially about the Sudeten crisis and the 1938 Munich conference, including but not limited to the individuals involved as they pertain to the conference (Chamberlain, Hitler, Daladier, Mussolini, and Czechoslovak leaders such as Beneš), up to Chamberlain’s “peace in our time” statement, focusing on the events and the Czechoslovak situation rather than the broader policy of appeasement toward Germany.  

\* \*\*Nazi-Soviet Pact (Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact) and its fallout:\*\* This chunk is substantially about the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, Hitler’s uneasy non-aggression pact with Stalin that shocked the West, and its fallout, including but not limited to: the implications for Poland, the Soviet takeover of the Baltic states, the Winter War with Finland, and the launch of Operation Barbarossa in June 1941\\. Focus on where the passage connects those events to the pact.  

\* \*\*US shift from neutrality to intervention:\*\* This chunk is substantially focused on the U.S.’s initial refusal to become entangled in European affairs, F.D.R.’s campaign on that promise, but the U.S. increasingly seeing the Presidency go against Congress’s explicit resolve to stay neutral and steadily promising more support to the Allies, through identifying loopholes in the Neutrality Act. Its pursuit of measures including but not limited to Lend-Lease, as it pertains to the U.S.’s abandonment of total neutrality, and eventually its declaration of war on not just Japan, but Germany and Italy in response to Hitler’s and Mussolini’s declarations of war on the U.S.  

\* \*\*Japanese expansionism and the road to the Pacific war:\*\* This chunk is substantially about Japan’s push to build an empire from 1931 to 1942: invading Manchuria and China, fighting border battles with the Soviet Union, moving into French Indochina, and then grabbing Dutch, French, British, and U.S. colonial territory across the Pacific and Southeast Asia, including but not limited to Indonesia, Malaya, Burma (now Myanmar), and the Philippines.  

\* \*\*Allied coalition diplomacy (Tehran, Yalta):\*\* This chunk is substantially focused on the alliance between the five Allies: the U.S., the U.S.S.R., Britain, France, and China. Any combination of two or more Allies coordinating their efforts to combat the Axis is fair game, including but not limited to the Normandy landings, Allied support for Chiang Kai-shek in the war against Japan on China’s home front (Stilwell backing Chiang, for instance), Allied cooperation with de Gaulle’s Free French and the French Resistance, or the Tehran and Yalta conferences.  

\* \*\*Postwar settlement planning:\*\* This chunk is substantially about postwar settlement planning between the Allies (through 1945), at conferences such as Bretton Woods, Dumbarton Oaks, Yalta, San Francisco, and Potsdam, as the meetings pertain to specific aspects of the postwar world, including but not limited to: the occupation of Germany, borders, demilitarization and denazification, the United Nations system, the Bretton Woods economic system, and Japan’s surrender terms and occupation. The Allies worked together to win the war; this anchor is about the Allies coordinating with one another on the world after.
