A02 Siege and encirclement warfare: the chunk is substantially about large-scale sieges and encirclement campaigns on any front of the war, including but not limited to those typically involving hundreds of thousands of troops and ending in mass casualties or the capture of large numbers of soldiers and prisoners of war.

A04 Battle of the Atlantic and convoy warfare: the chunk is substantially about the naval struggle (1939-45) over Allied shipping across the Atlantic, including but not limited to German U-boats and surface raiders attacking merchant convoys carrying supplies to Britain and the Soviet Union, and Allied navies, merchant crews, and aircraft working to protect them.
A05 Strategic bombing as an operational campaign: the chunk is substantially about sustained air campaigns against enemy cities, industry, and infrastructure (1939-45), including but not limited to the Blitz, the Allied bombing of Germany, and the firebombing of Japan, focusing on planning, targets, forces, and operational results, rather than close air support for ground troops or artillery bombardment.
A06 D-Day and the Normandy landings: the chunk is substantially about the Allied invasion of Normandy (June 1944) as the opening of a second front in Western Europe, including but not limited to its operational objectives, the strategic planning and breakdown of the landings, intelligence support specific to the invasion (French Resistance assistance to the Allies, the deception operation that led the Germans to expect an attack at Calais), Stalin’s interest in the second front as it relates to the invasion, and the results of the landings.
A07 Appeasement of Germany in the 1930s: This chunk is substantially focused on the policy of appeasement adopted by the future Allies towards Hitler’s efforts to flout the Versailles Treaty, including but not limited to rearmament and treaty renegotiation, a desire for Lebensraum, and aggressive actions taken towards countries such as Austria, Czechoslovakia, etc.
A08 Munich Agreement and Czechoslovakia: This chunk is substantially about the Sudeten crisis and the 1938 Munich conference, including but not limited to the individuals involved as they pertain to the conference (Chamberlain, Hitler, Daladier, Mussolini, and Czechoslovak leaders such as Beneš), up to Chamberlain’s “peace in our time” statement, focusing on the events and the Czechoslovak situation rather than the broader policy of appeasement toward Germany.
A09 Nazi-Soviet Pact (Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact) and its fallout: This chunk is substantially about the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, Hitler’s uneasy non-aggression pact with Stalin that shocked the West, and its fallout, including but not limited to: the implications for Poland, the Soviet takeover of the Baltic states, the Winter War with Finland, and the launch of Operation Barbarossa in June 1941. Focus on where the passage connects those events to the pact.
A10 US shift from neutrality to intervention: This chunk is substantially focused on the U.S.’s initial refusal to become entangled in European affairs, F.D.R.’s campaign on that promise, but the U.S. increasingly seeing the Presidency go against Congress’s explicit resolve to stay neutral and steadily promising more support to the Allies, through identifying loopholes in the Neutrality Act. Its pursuit of measures including but not limited to Lend-Lease, as it pertains to the U.S.’s abandonment of total neutrality, and eventually its declaration of war on not just Japan, but Germany and Italy in response to Hitler’s and Mussolini’s declarations of war on the U.S.
A11 Japanese expansionism and the road to the Pacific war: This chunk is substantially about Japan’s push to build an empire from 1931 to 1942: invading Manchuria and China, fighting border battles with the Soviet Union, moving into French Indochina, and then grabbing Dutch, French, British, and U.S. colonial territory across the Pacific and Southeast Asia, including but not limited to Indonesia, Malaya, Burma (now Myanmar), and the Philippines.
A12 Allied coalition diplomacy (Tehran, Yalta): This chunk is substantially focused on the alliance between the five Allies: the U.S., the U.S.S.R., Britain, France, and China. Any combination of two or more Allies coordinating their efforts to combat the Axis is fair game, including but not limited to the Normandy landings, Allied support for Chiang Kai-shek in the war against Japan on China’s home front (Stilwell backing Chiang, for instance), Allied cooperation with de Gaulle’s Free French and the French Resistance, or the Tehran and Yalta conferences.
A13 Postwar settlement planning: This chunk is substantially about postwar settlement planning between the Allies (through 1945), at conferences such as Bretton Woods, Dumbarton Oaks, Yalta, San Francisco, and Potsdam, as the meetings pertain to specific aspects of the postwar world, including but not limited to: the occupation of Germany, borders, demilitarization and denazification, the United Nations system, the Bretton Woods economic system, and Japan’s surrender terms and occupation. The Allies worked together to win the war; this anchor is about the Allies coordinating with one another on the world after.
A14 Persecution of Jews before the war: This chunk is substantially about the persecution and victimization of Jews before the war (up to September 1939), starting with Jews being scapegoated for Germany’s loss in World War I and escalating under the Nazi regime, including but not limited to: the 1933 boycott, the Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht, the refugee crisis, and the early groundwork of the Final Solution machinery (Heydrich, Eichmann). That includes the persecution of Jews in Austria after the Anschluss and in the Sudetenland after its annexation. Antisemitism elsewhere counts too, in countries with a Jewish diaspora like Britain, France, and many Eastern European countries, and in the United States, including Henry Ford, whose writings were circulated in Germany and admired by Hitler, and the second Ku Klux Klan.
A15 The Holocaust and extermination camps: This chunk is substantially about Nazi Germany’s Final Solution to the Jewish Question and the camp system behind it, including but not limited to the extermination camps (Auschwitz, Treblinka, Sobibor, and others) alongside concentration camps like Dachau and Buchenwald that had existed since the 1930s. It covers the actual nature of the camps and the ruthless purge of Jews: businesses driven out entirely, Jews forced into labor for German companies, possessions stripped and assets seized, and the systematic killing, rape, and other atrocities committed against Jewish people, including but not limited to the mass shootings, ghettos, and deportations that fed the camps, and the Nazi medical experiments carried out on Jews and other prisoners. This anchor does not cover the internment of Japanese Americans or other non-Nazi detention systems, which are covered by their own anchors.
A16 Occupation policy and civilian reprisals: This chunk is substantially about how the Axis powers ran occupied territory and treated the civilians under them. In Europe, that includes but isn’t limited to the German occupation of the USSR, Poland, France (and Vichy alongside it), and the Balkans, and Italy’s occupation of Albania. In Asia, it includes but isn’t limited to Japan’s occupation of Manchuria, China, and Southeast Asia, including the massacres of Chinese civilians at Shanghai and Nanjing, the Sook Ching massacre in Singapore, and the comfort women system. It covers the rules imposed on civilians, such as ghettos and forced armbands with the Star of David for Jews, along with collective punishment and reprisals against civilian populations.
A17 Collaboration vs. resistance in occupied Europe: This chunk is substantially about how people and governments in Axis-occupied Europe either collaborated with or resisted their occupiers, including but not limited to the following. The Vichy France regime was built under the leadership of WW1 French hero Pétain as a way for Germany to have an ally in France, with Northern France remaining under German occupation, as well as the La Résistance efforts in France subsequently supported by British intelligence and a British-backed Charles de Gaulle. It also covers resistance efforts in Poland, including the 1943 Warsaw Ghetto Uprising and the 1944 Warsaw Uprising, not to mention Polish RAF pilots defending Britain and Polish codebreakers helping crack Enigma before Alan Turing and the Bletchley team. In the USSR, it counts only where it’s about partisan resistance, and collaboration, in German-occupied Soviet territory, not the Red Army’s war. We also recall the resistance to Italian and German rule in Yugoslavia and Greece (the partisans), and Norway, where there was both a collaborationist government and real resistance.
A18 Treatment of prisoners of war: This chunk is substantially about how captured soldiers were treated by their captors during the war, including but not limited to: German atrocities against Soviet, Polish, and French POWs (the Soviets faced the worst treatment by far), the generally better, though not spotless, treatment of American and British POWs, Japan’s brutal treatment of Allied POWs (the Bataan Death March, the Burma Railway), the Soviet killing of Polish officers at Katyn, and the Allied killing of some German POWs. It also covers how far the Geneva and Hague conventions were followed, and cases like Stalin refusing to trade for his son Yakov.
A19 Forced labor: This chunk is substantially about the use of coerced labor as a system during the war, including its recruitment, transport, conditions, and economic use. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the millions of foreign workers pressed into the German war economy (Ostarbeiter, the French STO draft), camp labor supplied to armaments producers and firms like Volkswagen, Siemens, Krupp, and IG Farben, underground V-weapon production at Mittelbau-Dora, Japan’s use of Asian laborers and Allied POWs for companies like Mitsubishi and on projects like the Burma-Siam Railway, and the Soviet Gulag, one of the largest labor systems in history. A chunk that mentions forced labor only in passing, as one atrocity among others, doesn’t count.
A20 War crimes prosecution (Nuremberg, Tokyo): This chunk is substantially about the legal reckoning for wartime acts, including but not limited to: the Nuremberg International Military Tribunal (prosecuted by the U.S., Britain, France, and the USSR, with Göring, Hess, and Ribbentrop among the defendants) and the subsequent trials (the Doctors’ Trial, the Krupp and IG Farben trials), trials of camp staff like the Belsen Trial, the Tokyo tribunal (Tōjō among those convicted), and other trials of Japanese officers in Asia, such as Homma and Yamashita in Manila. It covers the concepts of crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity that these trials defined, and debates over them, such as “victors’ justice” and the “following orders” defense. It also covers perpetrators who escaped justice, like Mengele, and those who were caught and tried years later, like Eichmann, who fled to Argentina and was captured by Israel and tried in Jerusalem in 1961. The trials ran from 1945 into the 1960s, so this anchor covers them even though they fall after the wartime window.
A21 Mobilization of industry for war production: This chunk is substantially about how countries got their industry and economies cranking out weapons and materiel for the war, before and during it. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the U.S. “arsenal of democracy” speech and American homefront industry flipping over to war production, Germany scaling up its arms industry (the Luftwaffe buildup, for instance), Britain’s aircraft and fighter output, the USSR’s industrial buildup and moving its factories east of the Urals, China trying to equip its army and move its industry inland with some Soviet aid, and Japan’s push to grab the resources (oil, rubber, metals) it needed to fuel its war production, where the passage ties that to production.
A22 Lend-Lease and allied supply: This chunk should be substantially about the U.S.’s Lend-Lease program itself, which put the entire U.S. industrial might and manpower towards mobilizing tanks, aircraft, weapons, and whatever else to send to the Allied powers, along with food and oil, without needing an immediate cash payment. It was a way to really stretch the Neutrality Act passed by Congress, following Cash and Carry, but this anchor is about the supply, not the neutrality debate. The U.S. physically ended up producing these materials and sending them to the Allies, not just the UK and France, but a LOT of war material going to both the USSR and even to Chiang Kai-shek’s China, including but not limited to the Arctic convoys, the Persian Corridor, and trucks and food for the Red Army. The U.S. Merchant Marine, which saw the highest percentage casualty count, was the one daringly crossing enemy maritime limits to deliver the supplies; this counts only where the passage is about delivering Lend-Lease cargo, not a naval battle over a convoy. The U.S.’s enormous industrial mobilization counts only where the passage ties that production to sending it to the Allies. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: Roosevelt’s “arsenal of democracy” speech or factories retooling at home with no tie to shipping abroad (Mobilization of industry), a convoy battle told purely as U-boats versus escorts with no focus on the cargo (Battle of the Atlantic), or the congressional debate and vote on Lend-Lease that never gets to the supply itself (US shift from neutrality to intervention).
A23 Blockade and resource scarcity: This chunk is substantially about the specific blockades and resource scarcity that nations underwent during the war, including but not limited to Japan’s need for the materials to run its war after the U.S. embargo on oil (along with scrap iron and steel) and freezing of Japanese assets in 1941, the U.S. submarine campaign and mining that later strangled Japan’s shipping and left it short of fuel and food, and the British blockade of Germany cutting off imports like rubber, oil, and metals. It also counts China, with Japan occupying much of its front, including Chiang Kai-shek’s breaking of the Yellow River dikes in 1938, and the 1943 Bengal famine, but only where the passage is about the lost food and supplies, not blame or the human toll. The focus is on a country being cut off or denied what it needed, rather than on fuel shortages limiting a specific operation, Japan’s conquest of the colonies, or daily rationing at home. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: a convoy battle told purely as U-boats versus escorts (Battle of the Atlantic), Germany taking resources from occupied lands (Axis exploitation of occupied economies), an army or fleet stalled by a lack of fuel (Oil and fuel constraints), or families coping with ration books at home (Rationing and civilian austerity).
A24 Axis exploitation of occupied economies: This chunk is substantially about how the Axis powers took the money, resources, farms, and factories of the territories they occupied, rather than about how civilians were ruled or punished. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the massive occupation costs France had to pay Germany; Germany’s economic gain from Norway, including its role as the route for iron ore out of Narvik; Ukrainian grain, Romanian oil, and Soviet coal and industry; Czech and French factories producing for the German war effort; Belgian, Dutch, and Danish factories and farms; Poland; and Germany’s Hunger Plan, but only where the focus is on food being taken for the Reich. For Japan, it covers oil from the Dutch East Indies (Indonesia) and rubber and tin from Malaya, Manchukuo as a puppet state feeding Japanese industry along with the Japanese-controlled railroad running from the coast across Korea into China, and the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere as a way to supply Japan. Italy’s exploitation of occupied Albania and Greece counts too. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: workers being pressed into labor (Forced labor), ghettos, reprisals, or how civilians were ruled (Occupation policy and civilian reprisals), Jewish property seizure as part of the Final Solution (The Holocaust and extermination camps), Germany needing resources because of the British blockade (Blockade and resource scarcity), or arms production at home with no focus on taking from others (Mobilization of industry).
A25 Oil and fuel constraints on operations: This chunk is substantially about the constraint itself, meaning oil or fuel shortages limiting what an army, navy, or air force could actually do, rather than the blockade or embargo that caused the shortage, which can be labeled elsewhere. That includes, but isn’t limited to: Japan being constrained on expansion by oil, which split the Army (strike north into Soviet Russia) and the Navy (strike south for the oil fields) in 1941, with the Navy racing against its dwindling reserves; Germany’s invasion of the Soviet Union and its push for the Caucasus oil fields in 1942, which it never really got; Germany’s late-war fuel crisis, when its synthetic fuel plants were bombed and the Luftwaffe and its tanks could barely move, including the Battle of the Bulge, where it counted on capturing Allied fuel dumps; Italy’s navy sitting in port and Axis fuel shortages in North Africa when tankers were sunk crossing the Mediterranean; Japan’s fleet being stationed near its oil and the Yamato’s one-way mission in 1945; and the Allies, such as Patton’s advance stalling in 1944 when his gasoline ran short. Do not consider these examples as part of this section, unless they also focus on fuel limiting operations: the embargo or blockade itself (Blockade and resource scarcity), a chunk mostly about the bombing raids on oil plants (Strategic bombing as an operational campaign), or fuel mentioned as just one logistics problem among many on the Eastern Front (Eastern Front attrition and logistics).
A26 Aircraft and shipbuilding output: This chunk is substantially about how many aircraft and ships countries built and how fast, before and during the war. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the U.S. overtaking every other country on Earth in shipbuilding, even the historically dominant Britain, with Liberty ships coming off the slipways in record time and its carrier production ramping up enormously, from dozens of Essex-class fleet carriers to over 100 escort carriers built in a matter of months each, and Ford, General Motors, and Chrysler retooling their assembly lines to mass-produce airframes, engines, and parts, including fighters like the P-51 Mustang and the F6F Hellcat, with American factories making nearly 300,000 aircraft over the course of the war; Germany’s break from the Versailles limits on its shipbuilding, including the 1935 Anglo-German Naval Agreement (which let Germany build to 35% of Britain’s fleet, until Hitler tore it up in 1939) and the Z-Plan for a huge navy that was shelved once the war began, along with the Luftwaffe armament program, its U-boat building, and giant prestige ships like the Bismarck and the uncompleted Graf Zeppelin carrier; Japan’s Yamato and the end of naval treaty limits after the London Naval Treaty; and the RAF scaling up its own production of Spitfires and Hurricanes to match Germany, which is what defended Britain during the war, along with Britain’s King George V-class battleships. It also covers why Axis ship and plane production was throttled: chronic shortages of raw materials, Allied strategic bombing, and over-engineered, slow construction methods. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: the sinking of a ship or a dogfight told purely as a combat story, such as a Mustang escorting bombers, a carrier battle, or a King George V-class ship in action (Battle of the Atlantic, Pacific campaigns), factories retooling with no focus on aircraft or ships, such as tanks and general weapons output (Mobilization of industry), treaty or Versailles diplomacy with no focus on the actual building of ships or planes (Appeasement of Germany in the 1930s), or the design and tactics of one aircraft, like the Zero, without any focus on how many were built.
A27 Churchill’s rhetoric and public morale: This chunk is substantially about how Churchill’s rhetoric and public morale kept Britain’s fighting edge during its long stand against the Nazis without a major ally in Europe, with the Commonwealth and Empire still in the fight. It starts from Britain’s situation after the collapse of France, when Chamberlain stepped down in May 1940 and Churchill took over (Halifax having turned the job down), and Halifax and others pushed for peace, including sounding out Mussolini about terms with Hitler. Churchill held the line with speeches like “Blood, toil, tears and sweat,” “We shall fight on the beaches” (the “never surrender” speech to Parliament, which also warned that wars are not won by evacuations after Dunkirk and said the Empire, armed and guarded by the British fleet, would carry on the struggle), “Their finest hour,” and “The Few,” with Chamberlain’s Conservatives ultimately backing him. It also counts the royal family’s role in raising public morale: King George VI staying in London through the Blitz and speaking to the nation by radio, and his daughter Elizabeth, who broadcast to evacuated children and later trained as a mechanic and driver in the Auxiliary Territorial Service in 1945. It counts Churchill’s powerful oratory, fiery temperament, charisma, and boldness, and the backbone his speeches gave Britain’s commitment to defending both the mainland and the colonies when spirits and optimism were low. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: the Dunkirk evacuation told purely as an operation with no focus on Churchill’s words or morale, Churchill’s strategic planning or negotiations with the other Allies (Allied command coordination), the damage and daily life of the Blitz with no focus on him, the royals, or morale (Civilian experience of bombing and sheltering), or Chamberlain’s policy toward Hitler before the war (Appeasement of Germany in the 1930s).
A28 Hitler’s interference in military command: This chunk is substantially about Hitler overruling, micromanaging, or overriding his generals and commanders in ways that shaped military outcomes, whether or not the generals were right. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the Dunkirk halt order in May 1940; the switch of the Luftwaffe from bombing RAF airbases to bombing London in September 1940; the dispute in 1941 over Barbarossa’s priorities, with his generals wanting to go straight for Moscow while Hitler sent armor south toward Kiev and Ukraine’s resources; his “stand fast” orders at the gates of Moscow, the firing of generals, and his taking over as army commander in chief from Brauchitsch in December 1941; Stalingrad, where he split the 1942 summer offensive between the Caucasus oil fields and Stalingrad, sacked his chief of staff Halder, and forbade the encircled Sixth Army, led by Paulus, from breaking out; D-Day, where his control over the panzer reserves and his sleeping late kept them from moving while he believed Normandy was a feint; the Ardennes offensive, which he pushed through against his generals’ advice; and his orders in the bunker in the final days, including the “Nero Decree” and counterattacks by armies that barely existed. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: a battle told purely as a tactical narrative with no mention of Hitler’s decisions (Eastern Front attrition and logistics, Siege and encirclement warfare), Stalin’s interference with the Red Army (Stalin’s purges and the Red Army), command decisions among the Allies (Allied command coordination), or Hitler’s decisions on policy and ideology, such as the Final Solution, with no link to military command.
A29 Allied command coordination: This chunk is substantially about how the Allies’ commanders and command structure worked together, and where they clashed, rather than the diplomacy at the summit table. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the North African command under Eisenhower with Montgomery’s Eighth Army and Patton, who took over the U.S. II Corps after the Kasserine Pass defeat; Sicily, where Patton and Montgomery raced to Messina; the Italian campaign under Alexander with Mark Clark’s Fifth Army; Eisenhower’s appointment as Supreme Allied Commander with Bradley, Montgomery, and Patton under him, Tedder as his British deputy, and the Combined Chiefs of Staff behind it; the friction between British and American commanders over strategy (the Mediterranean vs. a cross-Channel invasion) and over a broad front vs. a narrow thrust, including Montgomery’s temporary command of U.S. troops during the Battle of the Bulge; and D-Day’s multinational command and beaches, with American forces at Utah and Omaha, the British at Gold and Sword, the Canadians at Juno, and the Free French commandos at Sword, with de Gaulle left in the dark until days before. In Asia, it covers Stilwell (“Vinegar Joe”) as Chiang Kai-shek’s chief of staff and U.S. commander in the region, Chiang’s dislike of him and his 1944 recall, and the Second United Front between Chiang and Mao against Japan, but only where the passage is about how the forces worked together or failed to. The Western Allies holding back Ultra and the atomic bomb from Stalin, and the timing of D-Day with the Soviet summer offensive, count only where they’re about coordinating command. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: summit diplomacy such as Tehran or Yalta with no focus on commanders (Allied coalition diplomacy), a battle told purely as a tactical narrative with no focus on who was in charge or how the Allies fit together (the campaign anchors), Hitler overruling his own generals (Hitler’s interference in military command), or Polish airmen and French Resistance fighters described only as resistance (Collaboration vs. resistance in occupied Europe).
A30 Stalin’s purges and the Red Army: This chunk is substantially about Stalin’s purge of his own military leadership and what it did to the Red Army, rather than the wider Great Terror. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the 1937 trial and execution of Marshal Tukhachevsky and other top officers on charges of treason, the killing of most of the marshals, army commanders, and corps and division commanders, and tens of thousands of officers purged, often for being too powerful or popular, as part of Stalin’s fear of rivals and demand for total loyalty; Beria, who took over the NKVD from Yezhov in late 1938 and ended the mass arrests, but whose NKVD shot a group of senior air force and army officers in October 1941 and released others like Rokossovsky; the view that the purge dramatically crippled the Soviet military command, as seen in the Red Army’s poor showing in the Winter War against Finland and in the initial months of Barbarossa, when Stalin’s generals were left inexperienced and some were shot afterward, like General Pavlov; and Stalin’s treatment of his own soldiers, including Order 270 branding captured soldiers as traitors, Order 227 (“Not one step back”) with its penal battalions and NKVD blocking detachments, and his refusal to trade for his son Yakov. It also covers Soviet manpower and the enormous human cost the Red Army bore in the Eastern Front’s attrition warfare, including the huge losses at Stalingrad and Kursk, and the large share of the war’s blood and toil that fell on the Soviets, where the passage ties it to Stalin’s leadership and the Red Army’s command. It also counts the later rise of commanders like Zhukov, where the passage ties it back to the purge. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: the wider Great Terror, such as the show trials, NKVD arrests of civilians, and the Gulag, with no link to the Red Army (Forced labor covers the Gulag), a battle told purely as a tactical narrative with no focus on casualties, manpower, or the leadership behind them (Eastern Front attrition and logistics), the Katyn massacre or German treatment of Soviet prisoners of war with no link to Stalin’s own policies toward his army (Treatment of prisoners of war), or Beria’s deportations of whole peoples and later atomic bomb work.
