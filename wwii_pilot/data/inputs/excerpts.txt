WWII Anchor Excerpts, Batch 1 (Campaigns; Politics & Diplomacy)
Each anchor has 15 excerpts: 1-12 are on-topic, 13-15 are hard negatives (they share keywords with the anchor but concern a different era or topic).

Campaigns
1. Eastern Front attrition and logistics
The German-Soviet war was marked by casualties on a scale unmatched in other theatres of the Second World War. By the end of 1941, approximately three million Soviet soldiers had been captured, yet the Red Army continued to field new formations raised from reserves.
The Soviet railway gauge of 1,524 millimetres differed from the standard gauge used in Germany and its occupied territories. German supply services were therefore obliged to convert captured track or transship cargo at the break of gauge, which restricted the tonnage reaching forward units.
During the autumn thaw of 1941, known in Russian as the rasputitsa, rain reduced the unpaved roads of the western Soviet Union to mud. Wheeled vehicles of Army Group Centre were frequently immobilised, delaying the advance on Moscow for several weeks.
German planning for Operation Barbarossa assumed that the Red Army could be defeated within a few months. As a consequence, the Wehrmacht entered the winter of 1941-42 without adequate cold-weather clothing or reserves of motor transport.
Between mid-1941 and 1942, Soviet authorities dismantled and relocated well over one thousand industrial enterprises to the Urals, western Siberia, and Central Asia. The relocated plants allowed Soviet industry to replace the tank and artillery losses of the first year of war.
In December 1941, temperatures in the Moscow sector fell below -30 °C, freezing lubricants and rendering many weapons and vehicles inoperable. German units lacking winter clothing suffered large numbers of frostbite casualties.
The distance from the German frontier of 1941 to Moscow was roughly one thousand kilometres, and the offensive of 1942 extended supply lines to the Volga and the Caucasus. These distances exceeded the capacity of the German railway and motor transport systems to sustain forward operations.
German army casualties on the Eastern Front reached roughly one million men by early 1942, and the replacement system could not restore divisions to authorised strength. Many infantry formations were reduced to a fraction of their establishment by the summer of that year.
Estimates of Soviet military deaths in the war against Germany range from approximately 8.7 million to over 11 million, depending on the method of calculation. The Soviet Union nevertheless drew on a population more than twice that of Germany to replace its losses.
Most divisions of the German army remained dependent on horse-drawn transport throughout the war rather than on motor vehicles. Horse losses in the winter of 1941-42 were severe, which further limited the mobility of infantry and supply columns.
From 1943, the Red Army received large quantities of American-built trucks under the Lend-Lease programme, with several hundred thousand vehicles delivered by the end of the war. This motor transport permitted Soviet offensives to advance far beyond their railheads.
German operations in the summer of 1942 were constrained by fuel supply, as the advance toward the Caucasus oilfields lengthened lines of communication faster than petrol deliveries could be increased. Panzer formations were at times halted for lack of fuel.
Hard negatives
During Napoleon's invasion of Russia in 1812, the Grande Armée outran its supply lines across the vast distances between the Niemen and Moscow. Disease, desertion, and the onset of winter during the retreat reduced the force by several hundred thousand men.
The Swedish campaign of Charles XII in Russia, culminating in 1709, depended on supply lines extending through Poland and Ukraine. The exceptionally severe winter of 1708-09 and the failure of expected reinforcements contributed to the army's defeat at Poltava.
On the Eastern Front of the First World War, the Great Retreat of 1915 forced the Russian army to abandon Poland and Galicia. Shortages of rifles and artillery shells, together with heavy losses of manpower, characterised the campaign.

2. Siege and encirclement warfare
The siege of Leningrad lasted from September 1941 until January 1944, a period of approximately 872 days. Estimates of civilian deaths, chiefly from starvation, range from about 650,000 to over 800,000.
On 19 November 1942, Soviet forces launched Operation Uranus, which encircled the German Sixth Army and elements of the Fourth Panzer Army at Stalingrad. Field Marshal Friedrich Paulus surrendered the remnants of the Sixth Army on 31 January 1943, and approximately 91,000 men entered captivity.
In September 1941, German armoured groups closed a ring east of Kyiv, trapping much of the Soviet Southwestern Front. German sources claimed the capture of more than 600,000 Soviet soldiers, one of the largest encirclements in military history.
The battles of Vyazma and Bryansk in October 1941 resulted in the encirclement of several Soviet armies west of Moscow. German accounts reported the capture of more than 600,000 prisoners, many of whom later died in captivity.
The Japanese conquest of Singapore culminated on 15 February 1942, when Lieutenant General Arthur Percival surrendered approximately 80,000 British, Indian, and Australian troops. The capitulation is often described as the largest surrender in British military history.
United States and Filipino forces on the Bataan Peninsula surrendered on 9 April 1942 after a campaign of about three months. Approximately 75,000 troops, many weakened by malnutrition and disease, became prisoners of war.
Tobruk was besieged by Axis forces for about eight months in 1941, with a garrison largely composed of Australian troops during the earlier phase. When the port fell to Rommel's forces in June 1942, roughly 33,000 Allied troops were taken prisoner.
In May 1940, the German advance to the English Channel encircled the Allied armies in Belgium and northern France. The subsequent evacuation from Dunkirk removed over 330,000 Allied troops, though most heavy equipment was abandoned.
The Falaise Pocket, closed in August 1944, trapped substantial elements of the German Seventh Army and Fifth Panzer Army in Normandy. Allied forces took tens of thousands of prisoners, and the destruction of German equipment opened the way to the Seine.
The siege of Sevastopol, from October 1941 to July 1942, ended with the capture of the Soviet naval fortress after prolonged bombardment, including by very heavy siege artillery. The defenders were gradually exhausted of ammunition and supplies as sea lines of supply came under air and naval attack.
Soviet forces encircled a German and Hungarian garrison in Budapest in late December 1944. The siege continued until 13 February 1945 and inflicted heavy civilian casualties and extensive destruction on the city.
In April 1945, American armies closed a pocket around Army Group B in the Ruhr industrial region. Approximately 317,000 German soldiers were captured, and Field Marshal Walter Model dissolved his army group and later took his own life.
Hard negatives
The Ottoman siege of Constantinople in 1453 lasted approximately seven weeks and ended with the fall of the Byzantine capital. Ottoman forces under Mehmed II used large bombards to breach the Theodosian Walls.
The siege of Vicksburg, conducted by Union forces under Ulysses S. Grant, ended on 4 July 1863 with the surrender of about 29,000 Confederate troops. The capture gave the Union control of the Mississippi River.
During the Franco-Prussian War, Marshal Achille Bazaine's Army of the Rhine was besieged in Metz and capitulated on 27 October 1870. More than 170,000 French soldiers were taken prisoner.

3. Pacific island and amphibious campaigns
Japan opened the Pacific War in December 1941 with near-simultaneous attacks on Hawaii, Malaya, Hong Kong, Guam, Wake Island, and the Philippines. Amphibious landings enabled Japanese forces to seize most of Southeast Asia within five months.
The garrison of Wake Island repulsed an initial Japanese landing attempt on 11 December 1941, sinking two destroyers. A second, larger assault supported by carrier aircraft overcame the defenders on 23 December.
United States Marines landed on Guadalcanal and Tulagi in the Solomon Islands on 7 August 1942, beginning the first major Allied offensive in the Pacific. The campaign lasted until February 1943, when Japanese forces evacuated the island.
The Battle of Tarawa in November 1943 demonstrated the difficulty of landing against a fortified atoll. Landing craft grounded on the coral reef, and the 2nd Marine Division suffered approximately 1,000 dead while nearly the entire Japanese garrison was killed.
The Allied island-hopping strategy bypassed heavily fortified Japanese positions such as Rabaul, which were isolated from supply rather than assaulted directly. The approach conserved forces and shortened the distance to the Japanese home islands.
The capture of Kwajalein Atoll in the Marshall Islands in early 1944 was completed by United States forces within approximately one week. Lessons from Tarawa, including more extensive preliminary bombardment and greater use of amphibious tractors, were applied.
The invasion of Saipan, begun on 15 June 1944, gave the United States airfields within B-29 range of Japan. The loss of the island contributed to the fall of the Tojo cabinet in July 1944.
At Peleliu in September 1944, Japanese defenders abandoned beach defence in favour of a network of caves and fortified ridges. The tactic prolonged the battle and increased American casualties, and was subsequently employed at Iwo Jima and Okinawa.
The assault on Iwo Jima began on 19 February 1945 and lasted over a month. Most of the roughly 20,000 Japanese defenders were killed, and American casualties approached 26,000, including approximately 6,800 dead.
Operation Iceberg, the invasion of Okinawa beginning on 1 April 1945, was the largest amphibious operation of the Pacific War. The campaign involved extensive kamikaze attacks on the supporting fleet and resulted in the deaths of an estimated 100,000 or more Okinawan civilians.
Japanese forces landed at Lingayen Gulf on Luzon on 22 December 1941 and advanced on Manila. General Douglas MacArthur returned to the Philippines with the landing at Leyte on 20 October 1944.
In the Dutch East Indies, Japanese amphibious forces seized oil-producing regions on Borneo and Sumatra in early 1942. The defeat of an Allied naval force in the Battle of the Java Sea on 27 February 1942 preceded the landings on Java.
Hard negatives
The Allied landings at Cape Helles and Anzac Cove on 25 April 1915 opened the Gallipoli campaign against the Ottoman Empire. The operation failed to secure the Dardanelles, and the peninsula was evacuated by January 1916.
The amphibious landing at Inchon on 15 September 1950, planned by General Douglas MacArthur, reversed the course of the Korean War. United States Marines and army units recaptured Seoul within two weeks.
The settlement of the Pacific islands by Austronesian-speaking peoples proceeded eastward through Melanesia into Polynesia over several millennia. Archaeological evidence from Lapita pottery traces an island-by-island expansion that reached Tonga and Samoa around 3,000 years ago.

4. Battle of the Atlantic and convoy warfare
The Battle of the Atlantic, lasting from September 1939 to May 1945, was the longest continuous campaign of the Second World War. Its object was control of the sea lanes carrying food, fuel, and war materiel to Britain.
Under Admiral Karl Doenitz, the German U-boat arm developed the Rudeltaktik, in which groups of submarines were concentrated against convoys on the basis of shore-directed reports. The tactic was most effective when escorts were few and air cover was absent.
After the fall of France in June 1940, German submarines gained access to Atlantic bases at Lorient, Saint-Nazaire, and Brest. Submariners later referred to the period through 1941 as the "Happy Time" on account of the high tonnage sunk.
Following American entry into the war, Operation Drumbeat in January 1942 brought U-boats to the eastern seaboard of the United States. Sinkings were severe until the introduction of coastal convoys in the spring of 1942.
Convoys were designated by route and speed, such as the fast HX series from Halifax and the slow SC series from Sydney, Nova Scotia. Escort groups of destroyers, corvettes, and frigates accompanied the merchant ships, which sailed in columns.
A region of the mid-Atlantic beyond the range of land-based patrol aircraft, known as the air gap, allowed U-boats to operate with relative impunity. Its closure through very long range Liberator aircraft and escort carriers was a decisive development in 1943.
Convoy ONS 5, which crossed the Atlantic in April and May 1943, was attacked by some thirty U-boats and lost about a dozen merchant ships. The escorts sank six submarines, a ratio that signalled a shifting balance in the campaign.
In May 1943, German losses of approximately forty submarines led Doenitz to withdraw his boats temporarily from the North Atlantic. The month is often termed "Black May" in the historiography.
Technological advances, including shipborne high-frequency direction-finding, centimetric radar, the Leigh Light, and the Hedgehog projector, increased the lethality of Allied anti-submarine forces. Intelligence derived from decrypted Enigma traffic complemented these measures.
U-boats sank approximately 2,800 Allied merchant vessels during the war, and approximately 30,000 British merchant seamen lost their lives. German submarine crews suffered losses of roughly three-quarters of those who served.
Convoy PQ 17, bound for Murmansk in July 1942, was ordered to scatter by the Admiralty on the belief that German surface ships were approaching. Twenty-four of its thirty-five merchant ships were subsequently lost to air and submarine attack.
Surface raiders also attacked convoys, as when the pocket battleship Admiral Scheer engaged convoy HX 84 in November 1940. The armed merchant cruiser Jervis Bay delayed the German ship, allowing most of the convoy to disperse.
Hard negatives
In 1917, German unrestricted submarine warfare threatened to sever British supply lines, with monthly losses exceeding 800,000 tons in April. The Admiralty adopted the convoy system in May 1917, after which losses declined.
From the sixteenth century, the Spanish Crown organised annual treasure fleets, known as the Flota de Indias, to carry silver from the Americas to Seville. The convoys were escorted by galleons against attack by English, Dutch, and French privateers.
The Convoy Act of 1798 made it compulsory for British merchant ships to sail under naval escort during the wars with Revolutionary France. The measure was intended to reduce losses to French privateers.

5. Strategic bombing as an operational campaign
The Blitz, the German night bombing of British cities, began in earnest on 7 September 1940 with a major attack on London. The capital was bombed on 57 consecutive nights, and the campaign continued in varying intensity until May 1941.
The raid on Coventry on 14 November 1940 destroyed much of the city centre and a number of factories. Approximately 550 civilians were killed.
The Butt Report of August 1941 examined bomb-strike photographs and concluded that only about one in three aircraft claiming to have attacked their target had bombed within five miles of it. The finding contributed to a re-evaluation of the effectiveness of RAF Bomber Command's night operations.
The Area Bombing Directive of February 1942 instructed Bomber Command to direct its attacks against the morale of the enemy civilian population, in particular industrial workers. Air Marshal Arthur Harris assumed command later that month.
The United States Army Air Forces pursued a doctrine of daylight precision bombing using heavily armed B-17 and B-24 aircraft. The doctrine presumed that formations could defend themselves against fighters without escort.
The raids on Schweinfurt and Regensburg of 17 August 1943 cost the Eighth Air Force 60 bombers, and a second attack on Schweinfurt in October lost a further 60 of 291 aircraft. The losses temporarily halted deep-penetration missions beyond fighter escort range.
The Combined Bomber Offensive, authorised at the Casablanca Conference in January 1943, coordinated British night operations with American daylight attacks. Its stated objective was the progressive destruction of the German military, industrial, and economic system.
Operation Gomorrah in July and August 1943 subjected Hamburg to a series of attacks that produced a firestorm. An estimated 40,000 persons were killed.
The introduction of the long-range P-51 Mustang escort fighter in early 1944 allowed American bombers to reach targets deep in Germany. Subsequent attacks on synthetic fuel plants and transport networks reduced the mobility of the Wehrmacht and Luftwaffe.
From June 1944, XX Bomber Command, and later XXI Bomber Command, operated B-29 Superfortress aircraft against Japan from bases in China and then the Mariana Islands. Under General Curtis LeMay, the command shifted in March 1945 to low-altitude incendiary raids by night.
The firebombing of Tokyo on the night of 9-10 March 1945 destroyed roughly sixteen square miles of the city. Estimates of deaths range from 80,000 to over 100,000, making it among the deadliest air raids in history.
The United States Strategic Bombing Survey, established in 1944, reported that German war production continued to expand until mid-1944 despite sustained attack. Its analysts concluded that attacks on oil and transportation proved more consequential than those on cities.
Hard negatives
German Zeppelin airships raided London and eastern England from 1915 onward, dropping incendiary and explosive bombs during the First World War. The raids killed several hundred persons in Britain over the course of the war.
Operation Rolling Thunder, conducted by the United States from 1965 to 1968, was a sustained bombing campaign against North Vietnam. It was intended to compel Hanoi to cease support for insurgents in the south.
The air campaign of the 1991 Gulf War opened on 17 January with attacks on Iraqi air defences, command facilities, and infrastructure. It preceded the coalition ground offensive by more than a month.

6. D-Day and the Normandy landings
Operation Neptune was the naval assault phase of Operation Overlord, the Allied invasion of German-occupied Normandy on 6 June 1944. The landings took place on five beaches designated Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, and Sword.
General Dwight D. Eisenhower served as Supreme Commander of the Allied Expeditionary Force, with General Bernard Montgomery commanding ground forces for the initial phase. The assault, originally scheduled for 5 June, was postponed by twenty-four hours owing to an unfavourable weather forecast.
Approximately 156,000 Allied troops landed in Normandy by sea and air on the first day, supported by nearly 7,000 naval vessels. The operation remains the largest amphibious invasion in history.
In the early hours of 6 June, the United States 82nd and 101st Airborne Divisions dropped on the Cotentin Peninsula, while the British 6th Airborne Division secured bridges over the Orne. Their objectives included protecting the flanks of the seaborne landings.
Operation Fortitude was a deception plan intended to convince the German high command that the main invasion would land at the Pas-de-Calais. It featured a fictitious First United States Army Group under General George S. Patton, together with misleading radio traffic and double agents.
French Resistance groups carried out coordinated sabotage of railways and telecommunications in the days surrounding the landings, according to plans such as Plan Vert. Coded messages broadcast by the BBC signalled the commencement of these operations.
German defences in Normandy formed part of the Atlantic Wall under Field Marshal Erwin Rommel, commander of Army Group B. Control of the armoured reserves was retained at higher headquarters, delaying their commitment on 6 June.
On Omaha Beach, the U.S. 1st and 29th Infantry Divisions encountered the German 352nd Infantry Division on commanding bluffs. Casualties among the assault waves were heavy, and the beachhead was secured only after prolonged fighting.
Two prefabricated artificial harbours, codenamed Mulberries, were towed across the Channel to support the landing of supplies. The port of Cherbourg was captured on 30 June, though its facilities had been extensively demolished.
At the Tehran Conference in late 1943, the Western Allies committed to a cross-Channel invasion in May 1944, a long-standing Soviet demand. Josef Stalin in turn undertook to coordinate a major Soviet offensive with the landings, which began on 22 June 1944 as Operation Bagration.
Allied casualties on D-Day are estimated at no fewer than 10,000, including more than 4,400 confirmed dead. German casualties on the day are less precisely documented, with estimates ranging from 4,000 to 9,000.
By the end of June 1944, over 850,000 Allied troops had landed in Normandy. The failure to capture Caen on the first day, however, delayed the planned breakout from the beachhead.
Hard negatives
In September 1066, William, Duke of Normandy, landed an invasion force at Pevensey on the south coast of England. His victory over Harold Godwinson at the Battle of Hastings on 14 October led to the Norman conquest.
In July 1346, Edward III of England landed an army at Saint-Vaast-la-Hougue in Normandy at the outset of a chevauchee through northern France. The campaign culminated in the English victory at Crecy in August.
Camembert is a soft, surface-ripened cow's-milk cheese that originated in the Normandy village of Camembert. Since 1983 the name Camembert de Normandie has been protected by an appellation covering production from Normandy milk.

Politics & Diplomacy
7. Appeasement of Germany in the 1930s
Appeasement was a policy of diplomatic concession pursued chiefly by Britain and France towards Germany during the 1930s, in the expectation that limited revisions of the Versailles settlement would avert another general war. It is most closely associated with Neville Chamberlain, Prime Minister from May 1937.
In March 1935, Germany announced the existence of the Luftwaffe and reintroduced conscription, in breach of the Treaty of Versailles. Britain responded with a diplomatic protest and, in June, concluded a naval agreement permitting Germany a fleet of 35 percent of British tonnage.
The remilitarisation of the Rhineland on 7 March 1936 violated both the Versailles Treaty and the Locarno Pact. France, lacking British support, did not respond militarily, though German commanders were reportedly prepared to withdraw if challenged.
A strong current of British public opinion favoured peace in the 1930s, shaped by the memory of the First World War. The Peace Ballot of 1934-35 recorded more than eleven million participants, a large majority supporting collective security through the League of Nations.
The Hossbach Memorandum, a record of a meeting of Hitler with military and foreign policy leaders in November 1937, outlined his intention to acquire living space in Eastern Europe. Historians have debated whether the document represents a concrete plan or a statement of general aims.
The Anschluss of Austria with Germany in March 1938 was accomplished by German troops entering the country at the instigation of Hitler. Britain and France lodged protests but took no action, regarding the matter as one of self-determination for a German-speaking state.
British military assessments in 1938 stressed the vulnerability of the country to air attack and the limited preparedness of its forces. Defenders of appeasement have argued that the policy gained time for rearmament, including the development of radar and the production of modern fighter aircraft.
The foreign secretary Anthony Eden resigned in February 1938 over Chamberlain's decision to open conversations with Mussolini's Italy. His departure removed a prominent sceptic from the cabinet and was followed by the appointment of Lord Halifax.
French policy was shaped by demographic losses in the First World War, a defensive military doctrine embodied in the Maginot Line, and reluctance to act without British support. Ministers such as Georges Bonnet favoured conciliation, while the alliance system in Eastern Europe weakened.
Winston Churchill, then without government office, criticised the rearmament of Germany and the weakness of British defences throughout the 1930s. His views were largely marginal until 1939, a period later described as his "wilderness years."
The historiography of appeasement was shaped by the polemic Guilty Men, published in 1940, which attributed blame to the British politicians responsible. Revisionist historians from the 1960s emphasised the constraints of finance, imperial defence, and public opinion.
The German occupation of the Czech lands in March 1939 undermined the premise that Hitler sought only the revision of Versailles. Britain subsequently issued a guarantee to Poland on 31 March 1939, marking a departure from earlier policy.
Hard negatives
In 991, following the Battle of Maldon, the English king Aethelred the Unready paid ten thousand pounds of silver to Viking raiders. Such payments, known as Danegeld, were repeated and substantially increased over the following decades.
In the fifth century, the Eastern Roman Empire made annual payments of gold to Attila, ruler of the Huns, in an effort to forestall invasion. The tribute was increased under the Treaty of Margus and later the Peace of Anatolius.
In ethology, appeasement behaviour refers to submissive signals by which an animal reduces aggression from a dominant member of its species. Examples include lowered posture, averted gaze, and grooming offered to higher-ranking individuals.

8. Munich Agreement and Czechoslovakia
In 1938, approximately three million ethnic Germans lived in the Sudeten border regions of Czechoslovakia, more than a fifth of the country's population. The Sudeten German Party under Konrad Henlein demanded autonomy in its Karlsbad Programme of April 1938.
On 15 September 1938, Neville Chamberlain flew to Berchtesgaden to meet Adolf Hitler, the first of three meetings that month. A week later at Bad Godesberg, Hitler raised his demands to the immediate cession of Sudeten territory and its military occupation.
Czechoslovakia ordered general mobilisation on 23 September 1938, calling up roughly one million men. President Edvard Benes relied on assurances of support from France, with which Czechoslovakia held a treaty of mutual assistance.
The Munich Conference, held on 29 and 30 September 1938, was attended by Chamberlain, Edouard Daladier, Benito Mussolini, and Adolf Hitler. Representatives of Czechoslovakia were not admitted to the negotiations and were informed of the outcome afterward.
The proposal presented by Mussolini at Munich had been prepared substantially by German officials. It provided for German occupation of the Sudetenland in stages between 1 and 10 October 1938.
The Munich Agreement transferred to Germany roughly 28,000 square kilometres of Czechoslovak territory, home to some three million Germans and a significant Czech minority. Czechoslovakia thereby lost most of its border fortifications and a substantial proportion of its heavy industry.
The Soviet Union had a mutual assistance treaty with Czechoslovakia dating to 1935, operative only if France also assisted. Soviet representatives were excluded from the conference, a fact that later influenced Soviet policy toward the Western powers.
Following the conference, Chamberlain and Hitler signed a declaration affirming the desire of their peoples never to go to war with one another again. Chamberlain returned to Britain and told a crowd outside 10 Downing Street that he believed the agreement had secured "peace for our time."
Edvard Benes resigned as president on 5 October 1938 and left the country. His successor, Emil Hacha, presided over a reorganised state in which Slovakia and Ruthenia received autonomy.
In the weeks after Munich, Poland annexed the Tesin region from Czechoslovakia, and Hungary received southern Slovakia under the First Vienna Award of 2 November 1938. These territorial losses further reduced the Czechoslovak state.
In Britain, the First Lord of the Admiralty, Duff Cooper, resigned in protest at the agreement, while the House of Commons endorsed the government's policy in a debate in early October. In France, Daladier, who had expected hostility, was greeted by cheering crowds on returning to Paris.
Czechoslovak military leaders regarded the Sudeten border fortifications as a strong defensive line against German invasion. Their cession, with the loss of the associated terrain, left the remainder of the country effectively indefensible.
Hard negatives
The 1972 Summer Olympics were held in Munich, West Germany, from 26 August to 11 September. The Games were overshadowed by the killing of eleven Israeli athletes and coaches by the Palestinian group Black September.
The Sudetes are a mountain range along the border of the Czech Republic and Poland, extending roughly 300 kilometres. Their highest peak, Snezka, rises to 1,603 metres.
The Prague Spring of 1968 was a period of political liberalisation in Czechoslovakia under Alexander Dubcek. It was ended in August when Warsaw Pact forces invaded the country.

9. Nazi-Soviet Pact (Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact) and its fallout
The Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact was a ten-year non-aggression treaty signed in Moscow on 23 August 1939 by the Soviet foreign commissar Vyacheslav Molotov and the German foreign minister Joachim von Ribbentrop. Its announcement surprised Western governments and the communist parties allied with Moscow.
A secret additional protocol to the pact partitioned Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence. Finland, Estonia, Latvia, eastern Poland, and Bessarabia were assigned to the Soviet sphere, while western Poland fell within the German sphere.
Anglo-French military talks with the Soviet Union in August 1939 foundered in part on the refusal of Poland to permit Soviet troops to cross its territory. Soviet negotiators, who had doubts about Western resolve, concluded an agreement with Germany instead.
Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, and Soviet forces entered the eastern part of the country on 17 September. A German-Soviet Treaty of Friendship and Borders on 28 September adjusted the demarcation line and placed most of Lithuania within the Soviet sphere.
Under commercial agreements of 1939 and 1940, the Soviet Union supplied Germany with grain, petroleum, and raw materials in exchange for machinery and manufactured goods. These shipments mitigated the effects of the British naval blockade on the German economy.
In the autumn of 1939, the Soviet Union compelled Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania to accept mutual assistance pacts permitting Soviet garrisons. In June 1940 the three states received ultimatums, and they were annexed as Soviet republics in August.
The Soviet Union attacked Finland on 30 November 1939 after Helsinki rejected territorial exchanges proposed by Moscow. Under the Treaty of Moscow of March 1940, Finland ceded the Karelian Isthmus and other territory, amounting to roughly a tenth of its land area.
In response to the invasion of Finland, the League of Nations expelled the Soviet Union in December 1939. The war revealed weaknesses in the Red Army that influenced German assessments of Soviet military capability.
In June 1940, the Soviet Union demanded the cession of Bessarabia and Northern Bukovina from Romania. The annexation of Northern Bukovina, which had not been named in the secret protocol, caused some friction with Germany.
In November 1940, Molotov visited Berlin for talks with Hitler and Ribbentrop on the division of spheres in Europe and Asia. The meetings produced no agreement, and in December Hitler issued Directive 21 ordering preparations for an attack on the Soviet Union.
Germany launched Operation Barbarossa on 22 June 1941, breaching the non-aggression pact. Soviet freight trains carrying raw materials to Germany were still crossing the border as the invasion began, and Stalin had disregarded numerous warnings.
The Soviet government denied the existence of the secret protocols until 1989. In December of that year, the Congress of People's Deputies condemned them and declared them legally invalid from the moment of signing.
Hard negatives
Frederick I, known as Barbarossa, was Holy Roman Emperor from 1155 until his death in 1190. He died while crossing the Saleph River in Anatolia during the Third Crusade.
The Partitions of Poland in 1772, 1793, and 1795 were carried out by the Russian Empire, the Kingdom of Prussia, and the Habsburg monarchy. They ended the existence of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth as an independent state.
The Treaty of Nystad of 1721 ended the Great Northern War between Sweden and Russia. Sweden ceded Livonia, Estonia, and Ingria, giving Russia control of much of the eastern Baltic coast.

10. US shift from neutrality to intervention
The Neutrality Acts of 1935, 1936, and 1937 prohibited the sale of arms to belligerents and restricted loans and travel on belligerent ships. They reflected a widespread view, reinforced by the Nye Committee hearings of 1934 to 1936, that American involvement in the First World War had been induced by financial interests.
In October 1937, President Franklin D. Roosevelt proposed in Chicago that aggressor nations be "quarantined" like the carriers of a contagious disease. The speech drew strong isolationist criticism, and Roosevelt did not pursue the proposal.
After the outbreak of war in Europe, Congress in November 1939 repealed the arms embargo and permitted belligerents to purchase weapons on a "cash-and-carry" basis. Because of British naval supremacy, the revision in practice favoured Britain and France.
In the presidential campaign of 1940, Roosevelt gave assurances that American troops would not be sent to fight in foreign wars. The statement was later cited by critics as inconsistent with his policy of increasing aid to Britain.
The Destroyers-for-Bases Agreement of September 1940 transferred fifty over-age American destroyers to Britain in exchange for ninety-nine-year leases on naval and air bases in British territories. Roosevelt concluded the arrangement by executive agreement, without seeking congressional approval.
The Lend-Lease Act, signed on 11 March 1941, authorised the president to supply war materiel to countries whose defence he deemed vital to the United States. Total American aid under the programme eventually amounted to roughly fifty billion dollars.
The America First Committee, founded in September 1940, mobilised opposition to intervention, with the aviator Charles Lindbergh as its most prominent speaker. At its peak the organisation had several hundred thousand members.
In August 1941, Congress extended the term of service of draftees under the Selective Training and Service Act by a single vote in the House of Representatives. The narrow margin of 203 to 202 indicated the strength of reluctance to expand military commitments.
In September 1941, following an encounter between the destroyer USS Greer and a German submarine, Roosevelt ordered American naval vessels to attack Axis warships found in waters deemed vital to American defence. The Atlantic patrols effectively placed the United States in an undeclared naval conflict.
The attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 led Congress to declare war on Japan the following day, with only one dissenting vote in the House. Germany and Italy declared war on the United States on 11 December, and Congress reciprocated on the same day.
Opinion polls conducted in 1940 and 1941 consistently showed large majorities opposed to American entry into the war while favouring material aid to Britain. This ambivalence constrained the administration, which framed assistance as measures of national defence.
The Atlantic Charter, issued by Roosevelt and Winston Churchill at Placentia Bay in August 1941, set forth joint principles for the postwar world, notwithstanding the formal neutrality of the United States. Critics in Congress argued that the declaration committed the nation to an alliance without legislative sanction.
Hard negatives
President George Washington issued a Proclamation of Neutrality in April 1793, declaring the United States impartial in the war between Britain and revolutionary France. The proclamation prompted a debate over the constitutional powers of the executive in foreign affairs.
Woodrow Wilson was re-elected in 1916 on a campaign emphasising that he had kept the United States out of the European war. The resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram contributed to the declaration of war on Germany in April 1917.
The Embargo Act of 1807, enacted under President Thomas Jefferson, prohibited American ships from trading with foreign ports in response to British and French interference with neutral commerce. The measure damaged the American economy and was repealed in 1809.

11. Japanese expansionism and the road to the Pacific war
The Mukden Incident of 18 September 1931, a staged explosion on a railway owned by Japan, provided the pretext for the Kwantung Army's occupation of Manchuria. Japan established the puppet state of Manchukuo in 1932.
The Lytton Commission of the League of Nations found that Japan's actions in Manchuria could not be justified as self-defence, and Japan withdrew from the League in 1933. The episode demonstrated the inability of the League to restrain a major power.
The Marco Polo Bridge Incident of 7 July 1937 escalated into full-scale war between Japan and China. Japanese forces captured Shanghai, Nanjing, and much of northern and coastal China over the following eighteen months.
Japan fought border engagements with Soviet forces at Lake Khasan in 1938 and Khalkhin Gol in 1939. The heavy Japanese defeat at Khalkhin Gol contributed to the decision to look southward and to the Soviet-Japanese Neutrality Pact of April 1941.
In November 1938, Japanese Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro announced a "New Order in East Asia." The Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere, a related concept, was publicised by Foreign Minister Matsuoka Yosuke in August 1940.
Domestic political violence undermined civilian control of Japanese policy in the 1930s. Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi was assassinated in May 1932, and an attempted coup by army officers occurred in Tokyo in February 1936.
Japan occupied northern French Indochina in September 1940 and southern Indochina in July 1941 with the acquiescence of the Vichy government. The United States responded by freezing Japanese assets and imposing an oil embargo.
Japan imported the great majority of its petroleum, with a large share from the United States. The embargo of 1941 encouraged Japanese planners to seek the oil of the Netherlands East Indies by force.
The Tripartite Pact, signed with Germany and Italy in September 1940, committed the signatories to mutual assistance if attacked by a power not then at war. The agreement was directed principally at deterring the United States.
On 7 and 8 December 1941, Japan launched coordinated attacks on Pearl Harbor, Malaya, Hong Kong, Guam, Wake Island, and the Philippines, and entered Thailand. Within months it had also occupied Burma and the Netherlands East Indies.
Thailand, after brief resistance on 8 December 1941, permitted passage of Japanese troops and concluded an alliance with Japan on 21 December. The arrangement enabled the invasion of Burma and Malaya from Thai territory.
Japanese strategic debate in the 1930s contrasted a northward strategy against the Soviet Union, favoured by much of the army, with a southward strategy toward Southeast Asian resources, favoured by the navy. The events of 1939 and 1940 weighted the decision toward the south.
Hard negatives
Between 1592 and 1598, Toyotomi Hideyoshi launched two invasions of Korea with the declared aim of conquering Ming China. Korean naval forces under Yi Sun-sin and Ming intervention frustrated the campaigns.
In 1644, Manchu forces under the Qing dynasty took Beijing and began the conquest of China proper. The Manchus had earlier unified the Jurchen peoples of the northeast, the region later called Manchuria.
The Indochinese Union was created by France in 1887 by combining Cochinchina, Annam, Tonkin, and Cambodia, with Laos added in 1899. The colony was administered by a French governor-general.

12. Allied coalition diplomacy (Tehran, Yalta)
At the Arcadia Conference in Washington, held from December 1941 to January 1942, Churchill and Roosevelt agreed that Germany would be defeated first. The conference established the Combined Chiefs of Staff to direct Anglo-American strategy.
On 1 January 1942, twenty-six states, including the United States, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, and China, signed the Declaration by United Nations. The signatories pledged to employ their full resources against the Axis and not to conclude a separate armistice or peace.
The Anglo-Soviet Treaty of May 1942 established a twenty-year alliance and bound both parties to postwar cooperation. Molotov, visiting Washington the same month, obtained from Roosevelt a statement that a second front might be opened in 1942, which was not realised.
At the Casablanca Conference of January 1943, Roosevelt and Churchill announced the policy of unconditional surrender for the Axis powers. Stalin declined to attend because of the Battle of Stalingrad, and the French generals de Gaulle and Giraud were brought together for a meeting.
General Joseph Stilwell served from 1942 as chief of staff to Chiang Kai-shek and commander of American forces in the China-Burma-India theatre. Persistent disagreement over the use of Chinese forces led to his recall in October 1944.
The Cairo Conference of November 1943 brought together Roosevelt, Churchill, and Chiang Kai-shek. Its declaration stated that Manchuria, Formosa, and the Pescadores would be restored to China after the defeat of Japan.
The Tehran Conference, held from 28 November to 1 December 1943, was the first meeting of Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin. The leaders confirmed an invasion of northern France for May 1944, and Stalin affirmed that the Soviet Union would enter the war against Japan after Germany's defeat.
The French Committee of National Liberation, established at Algiers in June 1943 under the leadership of de Gaulle and Giraud, claimed to represent France. Roosevelt withheld recognition for much of the war, and the Allies accepted de Gaulle's Provisional Government only in October 1944.
At the Moscow Conference of October 1944, Churchill and Stalin reached an informal arrangement on proportions of influence in the Balkans. The so-called percentages agreement assigned predominant Soviet influence to Romania and Bulgaria, and British influence to Greece.
The Yalta Conference of February 1945 was held at the Livadia Palace in the Crimea. The leaders agreed that France would receive an occupation zone in Germany, and that the Soviet Union would enter the war against Japan within two to three months of Germany's surrender.
At Yalta, the Allies issued the Declaration on Liberated Europe, committing them to the establishment of democratic governments through free elections. Subsequent disagreement over its application, especially in Poland, became a source of friction between the Soviet Union and the Western Allies.
Roosevelt advocated recognition of China as one of the "Four Policemen" who would maintain postwar order, alongside the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union. Churchill doubted China's capacity to fill such a role, and the issue illustrated differing Allied conceptions of Chinese power.
Hard negatives
The Triple Entente emerged in 1907 as an alignment of Britain, France, and Russia, following the Anglo-French Entente of 1904 and the Anglo-Russian Convention. It stood in opposition to the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy.
The Treaty of Chaumont, signed in March 1814, bound Britain, Austria, Prussia, and Russia in a Quadruple Alliance against Napoleon. Each power undertook to maintain 150,000 troops in the field until the war was concluded.
Yalta, on the southern coast of Crimea, developed in the nineteenth century as a resort for the Russian aristocracy. The Livadia Palace was built for Tsar Nicholas II as a summer residence and completed in 1911.

13. Postwar settlement planning
The Bretton Woods Conference, held in New Hampshire in July 1944, assembled delegates from 44 allied nations to design the postwar monetary order. It established the International Monetary Fund and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development.
Under the Bretton Woods system, member currencies were pegged to the United States dollar, which was convertible into gold at thirty-five dollars per ounce. The negotiations were dominated by the plans of Harry Dexter White of the United States and John Maynard Keynes of Britain.
The Dumbarton Oaks Conference, held in Washington between August and October 1944, produced proposals for a postwar international organisation by the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and China. Disagreements over the voting procedure in the Security Council and the membership of Soviet republics remained unresolved.
At Yalta in February 1945, the Allies agreed that decisions of the Security Council on substantive matters would require the concurrence of the five permanent members. The Soviet Union's request for sixteen seats in the General Assembly was reduced to three, representing the USSR, Ukraine, and Byelorussia.
The United Nations Conference on International Organization met in San Francisco from 25 April to 26 June 1945, with delegates from fifty nations. The Charter was signed on 26 June and entered into force on 24 October 1945.
The European Advisory Commission, established in London in 1943, drafted the protocols dividing Germany into zones of occupation. A protocol of September 1944 assigned zones to Britain, the United States, and the Soviet Union, with Berlin placed under joint administration.
At Yalta, the Allies agreed that Poland's eastern frontier should follow the Curzon Line, with Poland to receive compensation from German territory in the north and west. The final determination of the western boundary was deferred to a later peace conference.
The Potsdam Conference met from 17 July to 2 August 1945, with Harry Truman, Josef Stalin, and, after the British general election, Clement Attlee. Its protocol set out the principles of Germany's demilitarisation, denazification, democratisation, and decentralisation.
The Potsdam Agreement provided that Germany be treated as a single economic unit under the Allied Control Council. Reparations were to be taken principally from each power's own occupation zone, with the Soviet Union entitled to additional industrial equipment from the Western zones.
The Potsdam Declaration of 26 July 1945, issued by the United States, Britain, and China, set terms for Japanese surrender, including disarmament, trials of war criminals, and occupation until a peaceful government was established. Japanese sovereignty was to be limited to the home islands and minor islands determined by the Allies.
The Morgenthau Plan, discussed at the second Quebec Conference in September 1944, proposed the deindustrialisation and partition of Germany. Roosevelt and Churchill initialled a memorandum endorsing it, but it was soon abandoned amid opposition from the American war and state departments.
The United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration was established in November 1943 by forty-four nations to assist liberated populations. It provided food, medical supplies, and agricultural equipment to devastated regions in Europe and Asia.
Hard negatives
The Treaty of Versailles, signed in June 1919, required Germany to accept responsibility for the war, disarm, and pay reparations. Its first section established the League of Nations through the Covenant.
The Congress of Vienna of 1814-15 redrew the map of Europe after the Napoleonic Wars, restoring monarchies and establishing a balance of power. Its principal architects included Metternich, Castlereagh, and Talleyrand.
Potsdam, a city southwest of Berlin, served as a residence of the Prussian kings. Frederick the Great commissioned the rococo palace of Sanssouci there in the 1740s.
Human Rights
1. Persecution of Jews before the war
Antisemitic propaganda in Weimar Germany promoted the Dolchstosslegende, which attributed Germany's defeat in 1918 to betrayal by Jews and socialists on the home front. The claim was widely circulated by nationalist groups and became a staple of Nazi rhetoric.
On 1 April 1933, the Nazi government organised a boycott of Jewish-owned businesses, with SA men posted outside shops and professional offices. The measure lasted a single day but initiated the systematic exclusion of Jews from German economic life.
The Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service, enacted on 7 April 1933, removed Jews and political opponents from public employment. Similar restrictions soon extended to the legal, medical, and academic professions.
The Nuremberg Laws, announced on 15 September 1935, stripped Jews of German citizenship and prohibited marriage and sexual relations between Jews and persons of "German or related blood." Subsequent regulations defined who was classed as a Jew by reference to the religion of a person's grandparents.
Beginning in 1938, decrees required Jews to register their property and progressively transferred Jewish-owned businesses to non-Jewish owners, a process known as Aryanisation. The measures substantially reduced Jewish participation in the economy before the outbreak of war.
Following the Anschluss of March 1938, Austrian Jews were subjected to public humiliation, expropriation, and violence within days. Adolf Eichmann established the Central Office for Jewish Emigration in Vienna in August 1938, an institution that served as a model for forced emigration elsewhere in the Reich.
The Evian Conference of July 1938, attended by delegates of thirty-two countries, failed to produce significant increases in refugee admissions. Most participating governments cited economic conditions and existing immigration quotas.
The Kristallnacht pogrom of 9-10 November 1938 destroyed synagogues and Jewish businesses across Germany and Austria. Approximately 91 Jews were killed in the violence, and some 30,000 Jewish men were arrested and sent to concentration camps. The government then imposed a collective fine of one billion Reichsmarks on the Jewish community.
In October 1938, following the cession of the Sudetenland to Germany, Jews in the annexed territories were subjected to expulsion, property seizure, and arrests. Many fled into the remainder of Czechoslovakia, and others emigrated.
In Britain, the British Union of Fascists under Oswald Mosley adopted overtly antisemitic campaigning in the mid-1930s, culminating in the confrontation known as the Battle of Cable Street in October 1936. In France, the press and political figures of the right attacked Leon Blum, the country's first Jewish prime minister, in explicitly antisemitic terms.
In Poland, ghetto benches for Jewish students were introduced at several universities in the mid-1930s, and anti-Jewish economic boycotts gained support after 1935. Romania's brief Goga government of 1937-38 enacted a review of Jewish citizenship that deprived many Jews of their status.
In the United States, Henry Ford's newspaper The Dearborn Independent published antisemitic articles from 1920, later collected as The International Jew and translated into German. The second Ku Klux Klan, revived in 1915, also incorporated hostility to Jews alongside its opposition to Catholics and Black Americans.
Hard negatives
The Alhambra Decree of 1492, issued by the Catholic Monarchs of Spain, ordered the expulsion of Jews who refused conversion to Christianity. Estimates of the number who left the kingdoms range from tens of thousands to over one hundred thousand.
The Russian pogroms of 1881-84, following the assassination of Tsar Alexander II, spread across the southern provinces of the empire. The May Laws of 1882 subsequently imposed further restrictions on Jewish residence and occupation.
The term "boycott" derives from Charles Boycott, a land agent in County Mayo, Ireland, who was ostracised by tenants during the Land War of 1880. The word entered English usage within months through newspaper coverage.

2. The Holocaust and extermination camps
The Wannsee Conference, convened by Reinhard Heydrich on 20 January 1942, coordinated the participation of government ministries in the deportation of European Jews to the east. The meeting protocol listed approximately eleven million Jews across Europe as falling within the scope of the plan.
From June 1941, Einsatzgruppen and associated police battalions carried out mass shootings of Jews in the occupied Soviet territories. At Babi Yar near Kyiv on 29 and 30 September 1941, 33,771 Jews were killed according to German reports.
The first extermination facility to operate, at Chelmno, began killing in December 1941 using gas vans. The Operation Reinhard camps of Belzec, Sobibor, and Treblinka were established in 1942 in occupied Poland, with the primary purpose of killing the Jewish population of the General Government.
At Auschwitz-Birkenau, arriving prisoners were subjected to selection, with those deemed unfit for labour sent directly to gas chambers using Zyklon B. Approximately 1.1 million people were murdered at the complex, roughly one million of them Jews.
Ghettos established in occupied Poland, including those at Warsaw and Lodz, confined Jewish populations in overcrowded districts with minimal food and sanitation. Deportations from the ghettos to extermination camps commenced in 1942, and approximately 250,000 persons were sent from Warsaw to Treblinka between July and September.
Jewish businesses and property were seized through Aryanisation and, in occupied territories, by confiscation. Victims' personal belongings were collected at the camps, sorted, and shipped to Germany for distribution or sale.
Concentration camps such as Dachau, opened in March 1933, and Buchenwald, opened in 1937, were originally established to detain political opponents and other groups deemed hostile. During the war their populations expanded to include prisoners from across occupied Europe, with high mortality from disease, starvation, and forced labour.
Nazi physicians conducted medical experiments on prisoners at several camps, including Dachau, Auschwitz, and Ravensbruck, without consent and frequently resulting in death or permanent injury. Josef Mengele at Auschwitz performed research on twins drawn from the camp's prisoner population.
Jewish prisoners were also hired out as forced labourers to German firms, which paid the SS for the use of their workers. Many such labourers were worked until exhaustion and then selected for killing.
Deportations from Hungary began in May 1944 and, within approximately eight weeks, some 430,000 Jews were transported to Auschwitz. The operation, directed with the cooperation of Hungarian authorities, was among the most rapid phases of the genocide.
Sexual violence against Jewish women is documented in ghettos, in the course of mass shootings, and in camps, although its extent has been difficult to establish owing to the silence of survivors and the destruction of records. Historians have increasingly examined the subject since the 1990s.
As Soviet forces advanced in 1944 and 1945, the SS evacuated camps on forced marches known as death marches, during which tens of thousands of prisoners perished. Soviet troops liberated Auschwitz on 27 January 1945, finding approximately 7,000 surviving prisoners.
Hard negatives
During the Second Boer War, British forces interned Boer civilians in camps in South Africa between 1900 and 1902. Disease and poor conditions caused an estimated 26,000 deaths among Boer internees, most of them children.
In 1915, the Ottoman government ordered the deportation of Armenians from Anatolia to the Syrian desert. Massacres and death from starvation and exhaustion during the deportations killed an estimated one million or more people.
The Tuskegee Study of Untreated Syphilis in the Negro Male, conducted by the United States Public Health Service from 1932 to 1972, observed the progression of the disease in several hundred Black men. Participants were not informed of their diagnosis and were denied effective treatment after penicillin became available.

3. Occupation policy and civilian reprisals
Following the invasion of Poland, Germany annexed western territories and established the General Government under Hans Frank in October 1939. Occupation policy treated the Polish population as a labour reserve and targeted the intelligentsia for elimination.
In November 1939, the German authorities in the General Government decreed that Jews wear a white armband bearing a blue Star of David. Similar identification measures were introduced in other occupied territories in 1941 and 1942.
The Warsaw Ghetto was sealed in November 1940 with a wall enclosing some 400,000 persons in an area of roughly 3.4 square kilometres. Mortality from starvation and disease was high even before deportations began in 1942.
In September 1941, Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel directed that the death of a German soldier in the occupied East be answered by the execution of fifty to one hundred communists. The policy of hostage-taking and collective reprisal became routine in occupied Europe.
The village of Lidice in Bohemia was destroyed in June 1942 in retaliation for the assassination of Reinhard Heydrich. The male inhabitants were shot, the women were sent to Ravensbruck, and most of the children were killed at Chelmno or placed with German families.
At Oradour-sur-Glane on 10 June 1944, soldiers of the Waffen-SS Das Reich Division killed 642 villagers, including women and children. The reprisal followed Resistance activity in the region after the Normandy landings.
France was divided after the armistice of June 1940 into an occupied northern zone and an unoccupied southern zone administered from Vichy, with the Germans occupying the south from November 1942. The Vichy regime enacted its own anti-Jewish statutes in October 1940, independently of German demands.
Italy occupied Albania in April 1939 after a brief invasion and united the Albanian crown with that of the Italian king. Italian authorities later also occupied parts of Yugoslavia and Greece, employing internment and reprisals against civilians in counterinsurgency operations.
The Japanese capture of Nanjing in December 1937 was followed by several weeks of killing, rape, and looting. Estimates of Chinese deaths range from approximately 40,000 to over 300,000, depending on the definition of the area and period considered.
Following the capture of Singapore in February 1942, the Japanese military police carried out the Sook Ching operation, screening Chinese men for alleged anti-Japanese sentiment. Japanese postwar testimony cited about 5,000 deaths, while Singaporean estimates range from 25,000 to 50,000.
The comfort women system, established by the Japanese military from the early 1930s, placed women from Korea, China, the Philippines, and other territories in military brothels. Estimates of the number of women involved range widely, from tens of thousands to as many as 200,000.
In North China, the Japanese army conducted a campaign known in Chinese sources as the "Three Alls" policy against communist-held areas in 1941 and 1942. The policy resulted in extensive depopulation and destruction of villages.
Hard negatives
During the German invasion of Belgium in August 1914, troops executed approximately 6,000 civilians in reprisal for alleged sniper attacks. The burning of the university library at Louvain became a symbol of what Allied propaganda termed the Rape of Belgium.
During the American Civil War, Union forces occupied New Orleans in May 1862 under General Benjamin Butler. His General Order No. 28 and the execution of William Mumford for removing a flag provoked intense hostility among the city's civilians.
In January 1923, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr industrial region after Germany defaulted on reparations deliveries. The German government responded with a policy of passive resistance, contributing to hyperinflation in the following months.

4. Collaboration vs. resistance in occupied Europe
After the armistice of 22 June 1940, the French National Assembly voted full powers to Marshal Philippe Petain on 10 July, establishing the French State at Vichy. The regime pursued a policy of collaboration with Germany, formalised in part by Petain's meeting with Hitler at Montoire in October 1940.
Charles de Gaulle, in a broadcast from London on 18 June 1940, urged French soldiers to continue the fight. He subsequently organised the Free French Forces with British support and was recognised by the British government as leader of the Free French.
Pierre Laval, returning to power in April 1942, expanded cooperation with the German authorities, including the deportation of foreign Jews from the unoccupied zone. In January 1943 the Vichy government created the Milice, a paramilitary force used against the Resistance.
Resistance movements in France were unified under the National Council of the Resistance in May 1943, with Jean Moulin as the representative of de Gaulle. The British Special Operations Executive supplied weapons and agents to resistance networks.
The compulsory labour service (STO) introduced in February 1943 drove many young Frenchmen to join the maquis in rural areas. The maquis played a role in harassing German forces during and after the Normandy landings.
The Polish Home Army, the largest resistance organisation in occupied Europe, numbered several hundred thousand members at its peak. It operated under the authority of the Polish government-in-exile in London.
The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising began on 19 April 1943, when Jewish fighters of the Jewish Combat Organisation resisted the final deportations. The Germans, under Jurgen Stroop, suppressed the uprising by mid-May.
The Warsaw Uprising, launched by the Home Army on 1 August 1944, lasted 63 days and ended in capitulation. An estimated 150,000 to 200,000 civilians died, and the city was subsequently systematically demolished by German forces.
Polish pilots made up a notable contingent of the Allied air forces in the Battle of Britain in 1940, with No. 303 Squadron achieving the highest number of kills among units of Fighter Command. Polish cryptologists Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Rozycki, and Henryk Zygalski had broken the German Enigma cipher in the 1930s and shared their methods with Britain and France in July 1939.
In German-occupied Soviet territory, partisans were organised under a Central Staff of the Partisan Movement established in 1942. Local police auxiliaries recruited from the population assisted German authorities in antipartisan operations and the persecution of Jews.
In Yugoslavia, two resistance movements emerged: the royalist Chetniks led by Draza Mihailovic and the communist Partisans led by Josip Broz Tito. The Chetniks increasingly accommodated Axis forces in some regions, while the Partisans received Allied recognition by 1943.
In Norway, Vidkun Quisling was installed as minister-president in February 1942, heading a collaborationist government. Norwegian resistance forces, supported by the British Special Operations Executive, sabotaged the heavy water plant at Vemork in February 1943.
Hard negatives
The Kosciuszko Uprising began in March 1794 against the partitioning powers of Russia and Prussia and led to fighting in Warsaw in April. The insurrection was suppressed by November, and the Third Partition in 1795 ended the Polish state.
Vichy is a spa town in central France known since Roman times for its thermal springs. It developed as a fashionable resort under Napoleon III in the nineteenth century, and its mineral water is bottled commercially.
The term guerrilla entered European languages during the Peninsular War, when Spanish irregulars resisted Napoleonic occupation from 1808 to 1814. Small bands harassed French supply lines and couriers, drawing down large numbers of French troops.

5. Treatment of prisoners of war
Approximately 5.7 million Soviet soldiers fell into German hands during the war, and some 3.3 million of them died in captivity. The Soviet Union had not ratified the 1929 Geneva Convention on prisoners of war, a fact Germany cited to justify disregarding its provisions.
Soviet prisoners captured in the encirclements of 1941 were held in open-air camps with little shelter, food, or medical care. An estimated two million died in the first winter of captivity through starvation, disease, and exposure.
The Commissar Order of June 1941 directed that Red Army political officers captured by German forces be shot. Many Soviet Jewish prisoners were also selected out of prisoner-of-war camps and executed.
Some 1.8 million French soldiers were taken prisoner after the campaign of 1940, and about one million remained in captivity until 1945. French prisoners were generally subject to the provisions of the Geneva Convention, though many were assigned to labour in German agriculture and industry.
British and American prisoners held in German camps were generally accorded treatment in accordance with the Geneva Convention, with access to Red Cross parcels. Mortality among such prisoners was approximately 4 percent, compared with about 27 percent among Western Allied prisoners held by Japan.
In March 1944, fifty of the Allied airmen recaptured after a mass escape from Stalag Luft III were executed on the order of the Gestapo, in violation of the Geneva Convention. The Commando Order of October 1942 had likewise directed that captured Allied commandos be killed.
The Bataan Death March of April 1942 moved some 60,000 to 70,000 American and Filipino prisoners about 100 kilometres to prison camps. Prisoners were beaten, denied water, and killed by Japanese guards, and several thousand died en route.
Japan had signed but not ratified the 1929 Geneva Convention and treated Allied prisoners as dishonoured soldiers under its military code. About 60,000 Allied prisoners were employed on the Burma Railway, of whom approximately 12,000 died.
In the spring of 1940, the Soviet NKVD shot approximately 22,000 Polish officers and other prisoners at Katyn and other sites. The Soviet government attributed the killings to Germany until 1990, when it acknowledged responsibility.
Isolated incidents of Allied soldiers killing surrendered German prisoners are documented, such as the Biscari massacre in Sicily in July 1943. The incidents resulted in courts-martial of American personnel.
The Hague Convention of 1907 and the Geneva Convention of 1929 set standards for the housing, feeding, and labour of prisoners. Compliance varied greatly with the belligerent and the nationality of the captive.
Stalin's son Yakov Dzhugashvili, an artillery officer, was captured in July 1941 and died in Sachsenhausen in April 1943. Soviet authorities reportedly refused offers to exchange him for German prisoners.
Hard negatives
Andersonville Prison in Georgia held Union prisoners during the American Civil War from February 1864. About 13,000 of roughly 45,000 men confined there died of disease, exposure, and malnutrition.
During the American Revolutionary War, the British used prison ships such as HMS Jersey in New York harbour to hold captured Continental soldiers and sailors. Thousands of prisoners are thought to have died from disease and neglect aboard these vessels.
Following the armistice of July 1953, the Korean War ended with a prisoner exchange under Operation Big Switch. A significant number of Chinese and North Korean prisoners declined repatriation, an issue that had prolonged the negotiations.

6. Forced labor
By August 1944, approximately 7.6 million foreign civilians and prisoners of war were working within the German Reich, forming around a quarter of the labour force. Foreign labour was concentrated in agriculture, mining, and armaments production.
Fritz Sauckel was appointed General Plenipotentiary for Labour Deployment in March 1942 and directed large-scale recruitment drives in occupied territories. The methods employed included quotas on local administrations, roundups, and coercion.
Workers from the Soviet Union, known as Ostarbeiter, were required to wear an "OST" badge and were housed in guarded camps. They received lower rations and wages than Western workers and were subject to restrictions on movement.
In France, the Service du travail obligatoire (STO), introduced in February 1943, required young men to work in Germany. Approximately 600,000 French workers were sent under the scheme, while many others evaded it by joining the maquis.
IG Farben constructed a synthetic rubber and fuel plant at Monowitz, near Auschwitz, from 1941 using prisoner labour. The firm paid the SS for each worker, and the life expectancy of prisoners at the site was short.
Volkswagen's plant at Wolfsburg used thousands of forced labourers, including prisoners from concentration camps, Soviet civilians, and Italian military internees. Krupp, Siemens, and other firms similarly employed camp prisoners in armaments factories.
The underground factory at Mittelbau-Dora, in the Harz Mountains, produced V-2 rockets after the Allied raid on Peenemunde in August 1943. Approximately 60,000 prisoners passed through the complex, and an estimated 20,000 died from exhaustion, disease, and executions.
Organisation Todt employed forced labourers from occupied Europe on fortifications and construction projects, including the Atlantic Wall. Conditions varied but were frequently harsh, with workers drawn from prisoners of war and conscripted civilians.
The Burma-Siam Railway, approximately 415 kilometres long, was constructed in 1942-43 by Allied prisoners of war and Asian labourers. An estimated 90,000 Asian workers died, in addition to some 12,000 Allied prisoners.
Japan mobilised large numbers of Asian civilians as labourers, termed romusha in Indonesia, for military construction. Estimates of the total across Southeast Asia range from several hundred thousand to millions, and mortality was high.
Companies such as Mitsubishi used Allied prisoners of war in mines and shipyards in Japan. In 2015, Mitsubishi Materials issued an apology to former American prisoners of war, the first such apology by a major Japanese company.
The Soviet Gulag system expanded during the 1930s, and an estimated 18 million people passed through its camps and colonies between 1930 and 1953. Prisoners worked on projects such as mining, timber cutting, and canal construction, and were held in conditions that produced very high mortality in the early 1940s.
Hard negatives
In the Congo Free State, administered by Leopold II of Belgium, rural populations were compelled to collect wild rubber under a quota system enforced by militia. Atrocities, including the severing of hands, led to international condemnation and the Belgian annexation of the territory in 1908.
Convict leasing was practised in the southern United States after the American Civil War, whereby state governments leased prisoners to private companies for labour. The system, disproportionately applied to Black men, persisted in some states into the twentieth century.
The corvee royale in eighteenth-century France obliged peasants to provide unpaid labour on roads. The Controller-General Turgot attempted to replace it with a land tax in 1776, but the reform was reversed after his dismissal.

7. War crimes prosecution (Nuremberg, Tokyo)
The London Charter of 8 August 1945 established the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg and defined the offences of crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity. The tribunal's judges and prosecutors were drawn from the United States, Britain, France, and the Soviet Union.
The first Nuremberg trial opened on 20 November 1945 and concluded on 1 October 1946, with twenty-two defendants tried and nineteen convicted. Twelve were sentenced to death, and three defendants, including Hjalmar Schacht, were acquitted.
Hermann Goering was sentenced to death but committed suicide in his cell on 15 October 1946, hours before the scheduled executions. Rudolf Hess was sentenced to life imprisonment and remained in Spandau Prison until his death in 1987.
Defendants at Nuremberg argued that they had acted under superior orders. Article 8 of the London Charter provided that such orders did not relieve a defendant of responsibility, though they might be considered in mitigation.
Critics of the Nuremberg proceedings contended that the charge of crimes against peace and the principle of non-retroactivity raised problems under the doctrine nullum crimen sine lege. Supporters replied that the acts in question had already been prohibited by international agreements such as the Kellogg-Briand Pact.
Twelve subsequent trials were held at Nuremberg by United States military tribunals from 1946 to 1949. The Doctors' Trial convicted sixteen of twenty-three defendants for medical experiments and the killing of the mentally ill, and its judgment included the principles later known as the Nuremberg Code.
In the IG Farben trial of 1947-48, thirteen of twenty-four executives were convicted, principally for the use of slave labour and the plunder of occupied territory. Alfried Krupp was sentenced to twelve years' imprisonment and forfeiture of property in a separate trial, though he was released early in 1951.
The Belsen Trial, held by a British military court at Luneburg from September to November 1945, tried forty-five staff of Bergen-Belsen and Auschwitz. Josef Kramer, the camp commandant, was among those sentenced to death.
The International Military Tribunal for the Far East sat in Tokyo from May 1946 to November 1948 and tried twenty-eight Japanese leaders. Seven defendants, including Hideki Tojo, were sentenced to death and executed on 23 December 1948, while Justice Radhabinod Pal of India dissented from the judgment.
General Tomoyuki Yamashita was convicted by a United States military commission in Manila in December 1945 for atrocities committed by troops under his command and was executed in February 1946. The case established the doctrine of command responsibility and was controversial because the prosecution did not allege that he had ordered the acts.
General Masaharu Homma was tried in Manila for responsibility for the Bataan Death March and atrocities in the Philippines. He was convicted and executed in April 1946.
Adolf Eichmann was seized by Israeli agents in Buenos Aires in May 1960 and tried in Jerusalem in 1961. He was convicted and hanged on 31 May 1962, while Josef Mengele evaded capture and died in Brazil in 1979.
Hard negatives
The Leipzig war crimes trials of 1921 were held before the German Supreme Court following the First World War, after the Allies had sought to try nearly 900 Germans. Only a small number of defendants were convicted, and the sentences were widely regarded in Allied countries as lenient.
Peter von Hagenbach, governor of Breisach on behalf of Charles the Bold of Burgundy, was tried in 1474 by a tribunal of the Holy Roman Empire. He was convicted of atrocities against civilians and executed, a case sometimes cited as an early instance of command responsibility.
The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia was established by the United Nations Security Council in 1993 to prosecute serious violations of humanitarian law in the Balkan wars. It indicted 161 individuals before closing in 2017.

Economic & Industrial Output
1. Mobilization of Industry for War Production
World War II changed Evansville into a leading defense manufacturer, resulting in 80 factories transitioning to war production and manufacturing 330 different goods for the war effort. The military recognized the high production levels achieved by awarding 15 businesses in the region with Army-Navy “E” Awards.
In January 1942, Chrysler started producing military supplies and hired some five thousand new staff. Along with assembling airplane wings and tail surfaces, Chrysler manufactured three billion+ rounds of .45 caliber ammunition.
The appearance in 1944 of jet aircraft, impregnable and fast submarines, and V-1 and V-2, along with the impressive increase in armaments output through July, reveal how formidable was Germany's final challenge. The growing weight of Allied bombings Speer hoped to counter by dispersing Germany's most vital plants or by placing them underground, and he was making great progress in this direction.

4. Axis Exploitation of Occupied Economies

6. Aircraft and Shipbuilding Output
Of the ambitious Liberty ship program only seven ships were completed during 1941 and five of those were for the British. But the necessary groundwork had been laid for vast increases in shipbuilding which was to reach its peak in a year and a half.
Analyses of comparative performance by individual yards awarded contracts showed marked variations in efficiency. The best yards building Liberty vessels showed a production record for all deliveries during their operating history, of from two to three times that of the poorest yards.
In his address to Congress of January 6, 1942, the President said that he had ordered immediate steps to be taken "to increase our production rate of merchant ships so rapidly that in this year, 1942, we shall build 8,000,000 deadweight tons as compared with a 1941 production of 1,100,000. We shall continue that increase so that next year, 1943, we shall build 10,000,000 tons."
Aircraft production in 1944 not only surpassed the record of any previous year, but also ran closer to schedule than ever before. The industry overcame difficulties that had retarded the aircraft program since 1940.
The production of aircraft engines continued to increase, with a 1944 total of more than 259,000, an increase of almost 15 percent over 1943, and an increase of almost 400 percent over 1941. The emphasis shifted in the direction of larger horsepower and new models; the first deliveries of jet engines were included in the 1944 total.
German industry's vaunted production of 36,000 aircraft in 1944 was only 8,000 above what the Japanese produced that year. The numerical increase in 1944 over 1943, consisting almost entirely of fighters (a percentage rise of 55.9 percent), hides the fact that German production rose only 23.9 percent in terms of airframe weight.
Unhindered by Allied bombing, German production would have risen far higher and far faster. The target of 80,000-plus aircraft in production plans for 1945 gives an indication of the direction in which Milch and his planners were pushing.
The delivery of new fighters rose from 256 in April to 467 in September—more than enough to cover the losses—and Fighter Command emerged from the Battle in the autumn with more aircraft than it had possessed at the beginning.
