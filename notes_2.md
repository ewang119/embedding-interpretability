&#x20; ### \*\*Human Rights (7)\*\*



\* \*\*Persecution of Jews before the war:\*\* This chunk is substantially about the persecution and victimization of Jews before the war (up to September 1939), starting with Jews being scapegoated for Germany’s loss in World War I and escalating under the Nazi regime, including but not limited to: the 1933 boycott, the Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht, the refugee crisis, and the early groundwork of the Final Solution machinery (Heydrich, Eichmann). That includes the persecution of Jews in Austria after the Anschluss and in the Sudetenland after its annexation. Antisemitism elsewhere counts too, in countries with a Jewish diaspora like Britain, France, and many Eastern European countries, and in the United States, including Henry Ford, whose writings were circulated in Germany and admired by Hitler, and the second Ku Klux Klan.  

\* \*\*The Holocaust and extermination camps:\*\* This chunk is substantially about Nazi Germany’s Final Solution to the Jewish Question and the camp system behind it, including but not limited to the extermination camps (Auschwitz, Treblinka, Sobibor, and others) alongside concentration camps like Dachau and Buchenwald that had existed since the 1930s. It covers the actual nature of the camps and the ruthless purge of Jews: businesses driven out entirely, Jews forced into labor for German companies, possessions stripped and assets seized, and the systematic killing, rape, and other atrocities committed against Jewish people, including but not limited to the mass shootings, ghettos, and deportations that fed the camps, and the Nazi medical experiments carried out on Jews and other prisoners. This anchor does not cover the internment of Japanese Americans or other non-Nazi detention systems, which are covered by their own anchors.  

\* \*\*Occupation policy and civilian reprisals:\*\* This chunk is substantially about how the Axis powers ran occupied territory and treated the civilians under them. In Europe, that includes but isn’t limited to the German occupation of the USSR, Poland, France (and Vichy alongside it), and the Balkans, and Italy’s occupation of Albania. In Asia, it includes but isn’t limited to Japan’s occupation of Manchuria, China, and Southeast Asia, including the massacres of Chinese civilians at Shanghai and Nanjing, the Sook Ching massacre in Singapore, and the comfort women system. It covers the rules imposed on civilians, such as ghettos and forced armbands with the Star of David for Jews, along with collective punishment and reprisals against civilian populations.  

\* \*\*Collaboration vs. resistance in occupied Europe:\*\* This chunk is substantially about how people and governments in Axis-occupied Europe either collaborated with or resisted their occupiers, including but not limited to the following. The Vichy France regime was built under the leadership of WW1 French hero Pétain as a way for Germany to have an ally in France, with Northern France remaining under German occupation, as well as the La Résistance efforts in France subsequently supported by British intelligence and a British-backed Charles de Gaulle. It also covers resistance efforts in Poland, including the 1943 Warsaw Ghetto Uprising and the 1944 Warsaw Uprising, not to mention Polish RAF pilots defending Britain and Polish codebreakers helping crack Enigma before Alan Turing and the Bletchley team. In the USSR, it counts only where it’s about partisan resistance, and collaboration, in German-occupied Soviet territory, not the Red Army’s war. We also recall the resistance to Italian and German rule in Yugoslavia and Greece (the partisans), and Norway, where there was both a collaborationist government and real resistance.  

\* \*\*Treatment of prisoners of war:\*\* This chunk is substantially about how captured soldiers were treated by their captors during the war, including but not limited to: German atrocities against Soviet, Polish, and French POWs (the Soviets faced the worst treatment by far), the generally better, though not spotless, treatment of American and British POWs, Japan’s brutal treatment of Allied POWs (the Bataan Death March, the Burma Railway), the Soviet killing of Polish officers at Katyn, and the Allied killing of some German POWs. It also covers how far the Geneva and Hague conventions were followed, and cases like Stalin refusing to trade for his son Yakov.  

\* \*\*Forced labor:\*\* This chunk is substantially about the use of coerced labor as a system during the war, including its recruitment, transport, conditions, and economic use. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the millions of foreign workers pressed into the German war economy (Ostarbeiter, the French STO draft), camp labor supplied to armaments producers and firms like Volkswagen, Siemens, Krupp, and IG Farben, underground V-weapon production at Mittelbau-Dora, Japan’s use of Asian laborers and Allied POWs for companies like Mitsubishi and on projects like the Burma-Siam Railway, and the Soviet Gulag, one of the largest labor systems in history. A chunk that mentions forced labor only in passing, as one atrocity among others, doesn’t count.  

\* \*\*War crimes prosecution (Nuremberg, Tokyo):\*\* This chunk is substantially about the legal reckoning for wartime acts, including but not limited to: the Nuremberg International Military Tribunal (prosecuted by the U.S., Britain, France, and the USSR, with Göring, Hess, and Ribbentrop among the defendants) and the subsequent trials (the Doctors’ Trial, the Krupp and IG Farben trials), trials of camp staff like the Belsen Trial, the Tokyo tribunal (Tōjō among those convicted), and other trials of Japanese officers in Asia, such as Homma and Yamashita in Manila. It covers the concepts of crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity that these trials defined, and debates over them, such as “victors’ justice” and the “following orders” defense. It also covers perpetrators who escaped justice, like Mengele, and those who were caught and tried years later, like Eichmann, who fled to Argentina and was captured by Israel and tried in Jerusalem in 1961\\. The trials ran from 1945 into the 1960s, so this anchor covers them even though they fall after the wartime window.



&#x20; ### \*\*Economic \& Industrial Output (6)\*\*



\* \*\*Mobilization of industry for war production:\*\* This chunk is substantially about how countries got their industry and economies cranking out weapons and materiel for the war, before and during it. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the U.S. “arsenal of democracy” speech and American homefront industry flipping over to war production, Germany scaling up its arms industry (the Luftwaffe buildup, for instance), Britain’s aircraft and fighter output, the USSR’s industrial buildup and moving its factories east of the Urals, China trying to equip its army and move its industry inland with some Soviet aid, and Japan’s push to grab the resources (oil, rubber, metals) it needed to fuel its war production, where the passage ties that to production.  

\* \*\*Lend-Lease and allied supply:\*\* This chunk should be substantially about the U.S.’s Lend-Lease program itself, which put the entire U.S. industrial might and manpower towards mobilizing tanks, aircraft, weapons, and whatever else to send to the Allied powers, along with food and oil, without needing an immediate cash payment. It was a way to really stretch the Neutrality Act passed by Congress, following Cash and Carry, but this anchor is about the supply, not the neutrality debate. The U.S. physically ended up producing these materials and sending them to the Allies, not just the UK and France, but a LOT of war material going to both the USSR and even to Chiang Kai-shek’s China, including but not limited to the Arctic convoys, the Persian Corridor, and trucks and food for the Red Army. The U.S. Merchant Marine, which saw the highest percentage casualty count, was the one daringly crossing enemy maritime limits to deliver the supplies; this counts only where the passage is about delivering Lend-Lease cargo, not a naval battle over a convoy. The U.S.’s enormous industrial mobilization counts only where the passage ties that production to sending it to the Allies. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: Roosevelt’s “arsenal of democracy” speech or factories retooling at home with no tie to shipping abroad (Mobilization of industry), a convoy battle told purely as U-boats versus escorts with no focus on the cargo (Battle of the Atlantic), or the congressional debate and vote on Lend-Lease that never gets to the supply itself (US shift from neutrality to intervention).  

\* \*\*Blockade and resource scarcity:\*\* This chunk is substantially about the specific blockades and resource scarcity that nations underwent during the war, including but not limited to Japan’s need for the materials to run its war after the U.S. embargo on oil (along with scrap iron and steel) and freezing of Japanese assets in 1941, the U.S. submarine campaign and mining that later strangled Japan’s shipping and left it short of fuel and food, and the British blockade of Germany cutting off imports like rubber, oil, and metals. It also counts China, with Japan occupying much of its front, including Chiang Kai-shek’s breaking of the Yellow River dikes in 1938, and the 1943 Bengal famine, but only where the passage is about the lost food and supplies, not blame or the human toll. The focus is on a country being cut off or denied what it needed, rather than on fuel shortages limiting a specific operation, Japan’s conquest of the colonies, or daily rationing at home. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: a convoy battle told purely as U-boats versus escorts (Battle of the Atlantic), Germany taking resources from occupied lands (Axis exploitation of occupied economies), an army or fleet stalled by a lack of fuel (Oil and fuel constraints), or families coping with ration books at home (Rationing and civilian austerity).  

\* \*\*Axis exploitation of occupied economies:\*\* This chunk is substantially about how the Axis powers took the money, resources, farms, and factories of the territories they occupied, rather than about how civilians were ruled or punished. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the massive occupation costs France had to pay Germany; Germany’s economic gain from Norway, including its role as the route for iron ore out of Narvik; Ukrainian grain, Romanian oil, and Soviet coal and industry; Czech and French factories producing for the German war effort; Belgian, Dutch, and Danish factories and farms; Poland; and Germany’s Hunger Plan, but only where the focus is on food being taken for the Reich. For Japan, it covers oil from the Dutch East Indies (Indonesia) and rubber and tin from Malaya, Manchukuo as a puppet state feeding Japanese industry along with the Japanese-controlled railroad running from the coast across Korea into China, and the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere as a way to supply Japan. Italy’s exploitation of occupied Albania and Greece counts too. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: workers being pressed into labor (Forced labor), ghettos, reprisals, or how civilians were ruled (Occupation policy and civilian reprisals), Jewish property seizure as part of the Final Solution (The Holocaust and extermination camps), Germany needing resources because of the British blockade (Blockade and resource scarcity), or arms production at home with no focus on taking from others (Mobilization of industry).  

\* \*\*Oil and fuel constraints on operations:\*\* This chunk is substantially about the constraint itself, meaning oil or fuel shortages limiting what an army, navy, or air force could actually do, rather than the blockade or embargo that caused the shortage, which can be labeled elsewhere. That includes, but isn’t limited to: Japan being constrained on expansion by oil, which split the Army (strike north into Soviet Russia) and the Navy (strike south for the oil fields) in 1941, with the Navy racing against its dwindling reserves; Germany’s invasion of the Soviet Union and its push for the Caucasus oil fields in 1942, which it never really got; Germany’s late-war fuel crisis, when its synthetic fuel plants were bombed and the Luftwaffe and its tanks could barely move, including the Battle of the Bulge, where it counted on capturing Allied fuel dumps; Italy’s navy sitting in port and Axis fuel shortages in North Africa when tankers were sunk crossing the Mediterranean; Japan’s fleet being stationed near its oil and the Yamato’s one-way mission in 1945; and the Allies, such as Patton’s advance stalling in 1944 when his gasoline ran short. Do not consider these examples as part of this section, unless they also focus on fuel limiting operations: the embargo or blockade itself (Blockade and resource scarcity), a chunk mostly about the bombing raids on oil plants (Strategic bombing as an operational campaign), or fuel mentioned as just one logistics problem among many on the Eastern Front (Eastern Front attrition and logistics).  

\* \*\*Aircraft and shipbuilding output:\*\* This chunk is substantially about how many aircraft and ships countries built and how fast, before and during the war. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the U.S. overtaking every other country on Earth in shipbuilding, even the historically dominant Britain, with Liberty ships coming off the slipways in record time and its carrier production ramping up enormously, from dozens of Essex-class fleet carriers to over 100 escort carriers built in a matter of months each, and Ford, General Motors, and Chrysler retooling their assembly lines to mass-produce airframes, engines, and parts, including fighters like the P-51 Mustang and the F6F Hellcat, with American factories making nearly 300,000 aircraft over the course of the war; Germany’s break from the Versailles limits on its shipbuilding, including the 1935 Anglo-German Naval Agreement (which let Germany build to 35% of Britain’s fleet, until Hitler tore it up in 1939\\) and the Z-Plan for a huge navy that was shelved once the war began, along with the Luftwaffe armament program, its U-boat building, and giant prestige ships like the Bismarck and the uncompleted Graf Zeppelin carrier; Japan’s Yamato and the end of naval treaty limits after the London Naval Treaty; and the RAF scaling up its own production of Spitfires and Hurricanes to match Germany, which is what defended Britain during the war, along with Britain’s King George V-class battleships. It also covers why Axis ship and plane production was throttled: chronic shortages of raw materials, Allied strategic bombing, and over-engineered, slow construction methods. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: the sinking of a ship or a dogfight told purely as a combat story, such as a Mustang escorting bombers, a carrier battle, or a King George V-class ship in action (Battle of the Atlantic, Pacific campaigns), factories retooling with no focus on aircraft or ships, such as tanks and general weapons output (Mobilization of industry), treaty or Versailles diplomacy with no focus on the actual building of ships or planes (Appeasement of Germany in the 1930s), or the design and tactics of one aircraft, like the Zero, without any focus on how many were built.
