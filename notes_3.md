&#x20; ### \*\*Leadership (4)\*\*



\* \*\*Churchill’s rhetoric and public morale:\*\* This chunk is substantially about how Churchill’s rhetoric and public morale kept Britain’s fighting edge during its long stand against the Nazis without a major ally in Europe, with the Commonwealth and Empire still in the fight. It starts from Britain’s situation after the collapse of France, when Chamberlain stepped down in May 1940 and Churchill took over (Halifax having turned the job down), and Halifax and others pushed for peace, including sounding out Mussolini about terms with Hitler. Churchill held the line with speeches like “Blood, toil, tears and sweat,” “We shall fight on the beaches” (the “never surrender” speech to Parliament, which also warned that wars are not won by evacuations after Dunkirk and said the Empire, armed and guarded by the British fleet, would carry on the struggle), “Their finest hour,” and “The Few,” with Chamberlain’s Conservatives ultimately backing him. It also counts the royal family’s role in raising public morale: King George VI staying in London through the Blitz and speaking to the nation by radio, and his daughter Elizabeth, who broadcast to evacuated children and later trained as a mechanic and driver in the Auxiliary Territorial Service in 1945\\. It counts Churchill’s powerful oratory, fiery temperament, charisma, and boldness, and the backbone his speeches gave Britain’s commitment to defending both the mainland and the colonies when spirits and optimism were low. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: the Dunkirk evacuation told purely as an operation with no focus on Churchill’s words or morale, Churchill’s strategic planning or negotiations with the other Allies (Allied command coordination), the damage and daily life of the Blitz with no focus on him, the royals, or morale (Civilian experience of bombing and sheltering), or Chamberlain’s policy toward Hitler before the war (Appeasement of Germany in the 1930s).  

\* \*\*Hitler’s interference in military command:\*\* This chunk is substantially about Hitler overruling, micromanaging, or overriding his generals and commanders in ways that shaped military outcomes, whether or not the generals were right. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the Dunkirk halt order in May 1940; the switch of the Luftwaffe from bombing RAF airbases to bombing London in September 1940; the dispute in 1941 over Barbarossa’s priorities, with his generals wanting to go straight for Moscow while Hitler sent armor south toward Kiev and Ukraine’s resources; his “stand fast” orders at the gates of Moscow, the firing of generals, and his taking over as army commander in chief from Brauchitsch in December 1941; Stalingrad, where he split the 1942 summer offensive between the Caucasus oil fields and Stalingrad, sacked his chief of staff Halder, and forbade the encircled Sixth Army, led by Paulus, from breaking out; D-Day, where his control over the panzer reserves and his sleeping late kept them from moving while he believed Normandy was a feint; the Ardennes offensive, which he pushed through against his generals’ advice; and his orders in the bunker in the final days, including the “Nero Decree” and counterattacks by armies that barely existed. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: a battle told purely as a tactical narrative with no mention of Hitler’s decisions (Eastern Front attrition and logistics, Siege and encirclement warfare), Stalin’s interference with the Red Army (Stalin’s purges and the Red Army), command decisions among the Allies (Allied command coordination), or Hitler’s decisions on policy and ideology, such as the Final Solution, with no link to military command.  

\* \*\*Allied command coordination:\*\* This chunk is substantially about how the Allies’ commanders and command structure worked together, and where they clashed, rather than the diplomacy at the summit table. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the North African command under Eisenhower with Montgomery’s Eighth Army and Patton, who took over the U.S. II Corps after the Kasserine Pass defeat; Sicily, where Patton and Montgomery raced to Messina; the Italian campaign under Alexander with Mark Clark’s Fifth Army; Eisenhower’s appointment as Supreme Allied Commander with Bradley, Montgomery, and Patton under him, Tedder as his British deputy, and the Combined Chiefs of Staff behind it; the friction between British and American commanders over strategy (the Mediterranean vs. a cross-Channel invasion) and over a broad front vs. a narrow thrust, including Montgomery’s temporary command of U.S. troops during the Battle of the Bulge; and D-Day’s multinational command and beaches, with American forces at Utah and Omaha, the British at Gold and Sword, the Canadians at Juno, and the Free French commandos at Sword, with de Gaulle left in the dark until days before. In Asia, it covers Stilwell (“Vinegar Joe”) as Chiang Kai-shek’s chief of staff and U.S. commander in the region, Chiang’s dislike of him and his 1944 recall, and the Second United Front between Chiang and Mao against Japan, but only where the passage is about how the forces worked together or failed to. The Western Allies holding back Ultra and the atomic bomb from Stalin, and the timing of D-Day with the Soviet summer offensive, count only where they’re about coordinating command. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: summit diplomacy such as Tehran or Yalta with no focus on commanders (Allied coalition diplomacy), a battle told purely as a tactical narrative with no focus on who was in charge or how the Allies fit together (the campaign anchors), Hitler overruling his own generals (Hitler’s interference in military command), or Polish airmen and French Resistance fighters described only as resistance (Collaboration vs. resistance in occupied Europe).  

\* \*\*Stalin’s purges and the Red Army:\*\* This chunk is substantially about Stalin’s purge of his own military leadership and what it did to the Red Army, rather than the wider Great Terror. That includes, but isn’t limited to: the 1937 trial and execution of Marshal Tukhachevsky and other top officers on charges of treason, the killing of most of the marshals, army commanders, and corps and division commanders, and tens of thousands of officers purged, often for being too powerful or popular, as part of Stalin’s fear of rivals and demand for total loyalty; Beria, who took over the NKVD from Yezhov in late 1938 and ended the mass arrests, but whose NKVD shot a group of senior air force and army officers in October 1941 and released others like Rokossovsky; the view that the purge dramatically crippled the Soviet military command, as seen in the Red Army’s poor showing in the Winter War against Finland and in the initial months of Barbarossa, when Stalin’s generals were left inexperienced and some were shot afterward, like General Pavlov; and Stalin’s treatment of his own soldiers, including Order 270 branding captured soldiers as traitors, Order 227 (“Not one step back”) with its penal battalions and NKVD blocking detachments, and his refusal to trade for his son Yakov. It also covers Soviet manpower and the enormous human cost the Red Army bore in the Eastern Front’s attrition warfare, including the huge losses at Stalingrad and Kursk, and the large share of the war’s blood and toil that fell on the Soviets, where the passage ties it to Stalin’s leadership and the Red Army’s command. It also counts the later rise of commanders like Zhukov, where the passage ties it back to the purge. Do not consider these examples as part of this section: the wider Great Terror, such as the show trials, NKVD arrests of civilians, and the Gulag, with no link to the Red Army (Forced labor covers the Gulag), a battle told purely as a tactical narrative with no focus on casualties, manpower, or the leadership behind them (Eastern Front attrition and logistics), the Katyn massacre or German treatment of Soviet prisoners of war with no link to Stalin’s own policies toward his army (Treatment of prisoners of war), or Beria’s deportations of whole peoples and later atomic bomb work.
